Type D Personality: What the Research Supports for Hiring

3,004 words · 9 sections · 10 sources

A candidate came in quiet, tense, and slow to open up. Someone put a name to it: Type D personality. Now you have to decide whether that label belongs anywhere near the hiring decision. The traits behind it look almost exactly like ordinary interview nerves, and one conversation cannot tell the two apart.

Get the read wrong and you lose a strong candidate to nerves, or hire a weaker one who worked the room well. The label came out of heart-disease research. Nobody tested it against anything a job requires. The behavior is real, and you can manage it after hiring with job-relevant evidence and ordinary support.

What Type D Personality Means: Distressed, Not Disordered

Type D personality comes from medical psychology, not from HR, and the "D" stands for distressed 12. It describes two traits showing up together: negative affectivity and social inhibition 1. You only count as Type D when both run high 1.

Negative affectivity means feeling bad a lot, across time and situations: worry, irritability, gloom, anger, anxiety 1. Social inhibition means holding those feelings and opinions back around other people, for fear of being judged 1. Both stayed fairly stable when researchers measured them over four years. This is not a bad Tuesday afternoon.

Johan Denollet, a professor of medical psychology at Tilburg University, named the construct in 1995, building it from what he saw in cardiac patients 31.

That origin also fixes what the label is not. Denollet has been clear that Type D is not a psychiatric disorder or a sign of psychopathology 1. Someone scored as Type D may not meet the criteria for any clinical condition, depression included 1. Researchers keep warning that people read it as illness when it was meant as a normal personality type 2.

Keep one test in mind for any assessment: does it sort people into types, or does it measure what a specific role requires? Type D is the first kind.

Run any other type-sorting tool through the same test. We do that with the Enneagram in our guide to using it in hiring.

How the DS14 Scores Type D, and Why One Interview Can't

Researchers assign Type D with a scored questionnaire and fixed number cut-offs. Nobody reads it off a person's face. The tool is the DS14, and once you see how it works, you can see what an interview has no right to conclude.

The DS14 Is a Scored Self-Report Tool

The DS14 asks 14 questions and gives back two separate scores 4. Each answer is rated 0 to 4, so each score runs from 0 to 28. You count as Type D only if you hit 10 or more on both 4.

A Guarded Interview Is Not a Type D Finding

Here is the trouble. What defines Type D looks almost exactly like normal interview nerves. Anxiety, tension and guardedness are what most people bring into a room where they are being judged. A related trait, behavioral inhibition, means pulling back from unfamiliar situations, and a first interview with strangers is unfamiliar for nearly everyone.

What tells a trait apart from a bad hour is how often it happens, not how hard it hits. Descriptions of Type D stress that these feelings come up more often and more consistently over time than in most people. Everyone feels this way sometimes.

One conversation cannot show you what happens over time. That does not clear the quiet candidate either. It means you learned nothing at all. Score the interview on job-relevant behavior against a fixed rubric, and you learn something.

The Cardiac Studies Type D Came From, and What Broke

Even inside medicine, the label is shaky. Researchers built and tested Type D on heart patients, and the death-risk link that made it famous did not survive bigger studies. Every founding study ran on cardiac patients. None ran at work.

The early results looked dramatic. Among more than 300 cardiac rehabilitation patients, about 27% of the Type D group had died within 10 years, against 6% of the rest. Among nearly 900 coronary artery bypass patients, the Type D group was reported to be over four times more likely to have a heart attack or die within nine months of surgery.

Then bigger teams tried to repeat it. One analysis pooled more than 11,000 cardiovascular disease patients from 19 prospective studies and found Type D is not a risk factor for death in those patients. The smaller early studies "may have inadvertently reached exaggerated or false conclusions", the researchers wrote 5. A 2023 analysis found small samples produced bigger Type D effects than large ones, a pattern "possibly hinting at publication bias" 6.

So be more careful with the label, not less, before you drag it into hiring.

Why Type D Fails as a Candidate Screen

No Type D personality study has ever tested whether someone can do a job. Researchers built the label to predict heart events, death, and transplant rejection in patients with heart disease 2. Nobody has checked it against job performance or any hiring decision.

That matters because the label turns up dressed like a hiring tool. A name, a two-letter code, a tidy trait profile. That packaging is what pulls it into hiring talk, whatever the studies actually measured.

Where studies do report something about work, they report health and strain. One review found Type D linked to more work problems, including more absence-leave and higher vital exhaustion 7. Health psychologists measure strain. None of it measures whether someone is good at the job.

The clinical findings go further than most managers expect. A review of eight studies found Type D strongly linked to suicidal thoughts and behavior, across different countries and groups. That is mental health. You cannot screen for it in a hiring decision, and the law does not let you try.

Put the research aside and the everyday mistake is simpler. You read a quiet, tense candidate in the first few minutes, then the label shows up to explain the hunch. A read that fast is bias in a costume.

It cuts both ways. You pass on an excellent candidate who does not match the profile in your head, and you move ahead a weaker one who performed the right mood for the room.

There is a measurement problem too. When a job is on the line, people fill in a self-report questionnaire with whatever they think you want to read, so the answers are not always true. Test the work instead.

Where a Health-Linked Label Runs Into the ADA

Raising Type D in a hiring conversation is riskier than ordinary fit talk, because the label was built to flag health risk. Personality is not a protected trait, and courts have accepted that you can turn someone down over genuine fit concerns. You lose that cover the moment the trait stands in for an impairment you think you see.

A Health-Linked Label Can Look Like a Medical Inquiry

Before you make an offer, the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) lets you ask only whether someone can do the job, never whether they have a condition or how bad it is 8. The ban is wide. It covers any question likely to turn up a disability, including sideways ones about symptoms, treatment, or general mood.

The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) draws a clean line for personality tools. A test built to spot a mental disorder or impairment counts as a medical examination. A test of honesty, preferences, or habits does not.

Type D sits on the wrong side of that line. Nobody built it to measure traits at work. Its whole job is flagging cardiovascular and psychological risk, exactly the impairment territory that definition targets.

Courts have found that line crossed without anyone saying a clinical word. In Kroll v. White Lake Ambulance Authority, the Sixth Circuit held that ordering an employee into counseling could count as a medical examination once the record tied the order to mental health. Now read that back into your interview. Call a candidate guarded or distressed and act on it, and you are answering a psychological profile you imagined, not anything they did.

Unvalidated Screens Create ADA and Title VII Problems

The fix is a job link you can show. Courts want a clear line between what a pre-employment test measures and the tasks of that specific job. Type D has no such line. Its validated evidence runs to coronary disease, not to job performance.

You are also expected to check whether your hiring tools screen out qualified candidates with disabilities 9. Most employers skip it. In our topline report with SHRM Labs, 44.5% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners said nobody formally validates their assessments.

About 76% of organizations with more than 100 employees run some kind of personality testing. Testing everywhere with nothing checking it is how a normal hiring tool becomes a legal problem.

The ADA's "regarded as" prong widens the exposure. Since 2008, a candidate is covered if you act against them over an impairment you think they have, and they never have to prove a real limitation. You will not think you are screening for a health condition. That is the trap, because a label built in health research does it for you.

Behavioral Interview Questions That Score Actions, Not Mood

Swap the impression for a scored process. Ask every candidate the same job-relevant questions, then rate each answer against written behavioral anchors. Sackett and colleagues' 2023 meta-analysis puts structured interview validity at .42 10. Nothing else on its own predicts better, not cognitive tests, not work samples, not personality measures 10.

A 2025 meta-analysis by Wingate and colleagues covered 37 studies and 30,646 participants. It splits the prediction instead of giving one overall number: task performance at ρ = .30, and contextual performance, meaning citizenship behavior and team collaboration, at ρ = .28. The method is tested against work outcomes, not against personality types.

Structure also shuts the door a label like Type D walks through. Research in the Journal of Business and Psychology found standard formats cut bias effects by more than half against freeform conversations, d = .23 versus d = .59. Behavioral questions do the same job one question at a time. They ask what someone actually did in a real past situation, so you judge the doing, not the delivery.

Six steps build the interview:

  1. Pick the competencies the role needs, roughly five core ones, so every question is visibly about the job.
  2. Write 5-7 questions per competency set, phrased as past behavior: what happened, what you did, what came of it.
  3. Write the anchors before the interview. Never bend them afterward to fit how someone came across.
  4. Calibrate the panel in a 30-minute session: walk each question, agree what a 2 sounds like versus a 4, and score a mock answer together.
  5. Score on your own and write the rating down before anyone speaks in the debrief.
  6. When two interviewers land on a 2 and a 4 for the same answer, argue it against the anchor, not against impressions.

Every level in the rubric below describes something you can hear in the answer. Warmth and delivery have no way in.

Push on a thin or hesitant answer. The one rule is that a follow-up never changes the rubric you score against. A guarded first response that opens up under a good follow-up gives you the same evidence a chatty candidate handed over unprompted.

Our topline survey with SHRM Labs found 45.8% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners call their process inconsistent, unsure whether it picks the best candidate or the best interviewer. Writing your score down before anyone speaks is what stops that drift.

Type D Traits on the Job: Fit, Strengths, Risks

Once someone is on your team, you manage what you can see, not a diagnosis you are not qualified to make. The cluster is recognizable: sadness, irritation when work strays from the plan they laid out, a habit of spotting what is wrong first, hard self-talk, and steering clear of social events full of strangers.

The same profile has a second half most managers miss. These people tend to be organized, caring and thoughtful. Mess in the work environment bothers them, and they are often the last one still working on a task after everyone else has quit.

Fit comes down to the work. Low-risk work with standard, non-negotiable procedures suits them, and routine steadies them rather than grinding them down. High turnover or a team that keeps changing shape does the opposite, because constant change unsettles them.

On a team they bring systems and tools that drag messier colleagues into better habits, and they often steady everyone under pressure. Watch two things. They need to feel useful and appreciated, and since they hold so much in, people take advantage of that willingness to help. The second is quieter. In meetings where speaking up is the job, their silence reads as nothing to contribute.

Managing Type D Behavior Without Diagnosing It

If a new hire is reserved, expects the worst, or takes small setbacks hard, ask what is driving it rather than what to call it. Then help them toward more positive thinking, with training if that is what builds their confidence. Pushing them into counseling is not one of those moves. As Kroll above shows, once the record ties that push to mental health, it can count as a medical examination.

This takes time and patience. Part of the work is telling the person, plainly, that others in the organization value them.

Four moves do most of the work:

  • Match the task to the person: steady, procedural work in a low-turnover team that needs help and values it brings out their best. Watch the load, because that willingness to serve is the thing that gets over-drawn.
  • Ask what sits underneath: read withdrawal or gloom as a signal about workload, clarity or confidence. Ask before you interpret.
  • Set short-term goals: they sharpen what the person values and wants next, and teach them to look ahead with more hope, optimism and confidence.
  • Change the channel, not the person: if you need input they will not offer in a group, ask in writing or one to one, and use what you get.

This is ordinary management, done patiently, on behavior you can actually see.

Frequently asked questions

What are type A, B, C, and D personalities?

Four popular labels: A for driven and competitive, B for relaxed, C for detail-focused and conflict-avoidant, D for distressed. A and D grew out of cardiac research, while B and C are informal add-ons. None predicts job performance.

Is type D personality the same as introversion?

No. Introverts prefer quieter settings and less stimulation, while Type D means frequent negative emotion held back around others out of fear of disapproval 1. A calm introvert is not Type D.

What is the best job for a type D personality?

Ask that about work you already assign, not as a filter for your shortlist. Steady, low-risk work with clear standards suits this profile; constant change and high turnover do not. Test the skills, not the type.

What is the difference between type D personality and depression?

Depression is a clinical condition doctors diagnose against set criteria. Type D is a normal personality type, and the researcher who introduced it says it is not a psychiatric disorder 1. A Type D score does not mean depression 1.

Can type D personality change over time?

Both traits held fairly steady over a four-year period. Behavior still moves with support: training, short-term goals, and work that fits the person build confidence and a more hopeful outlook.

Type D personality belongs to health research, not to your shortlist, and one interview could never establish it anyway. What holds up is a scored process: the same job-relevant questions for everyone, rated against anchors you wrote in advance.
