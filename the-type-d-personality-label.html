Type D Personality: What the Research Supports for Hiring

3,129 words · 9 sections · 10 sources

A candidate came in quiet, tense, and slow to open up, and someone put a name to it: Type D personality. Now you have to decide whether that label belongs anywhere near the hiring decision. The traits behind it look almost exactly like ordinary interview nerves, so one conversation cannot tell the two apart.

Get that read wrong and you lose a strong candidate to nerves, or advance a weaker one who read the room well. The label came out of heart-disease research, and it was never tested against anything a job requires. The behavior it points at is still there, and it is manageable after hiring, with job-relevant evidence and ordinary support.

What Type D Personality Means: Distressed, Not Disordered

Type D personality is a construct from medical psychology, not a workplace category, and the "D" stands for distressed 12. It describes the co-occurrence of two traits: negative affectivity and social inhibition 1. A person is classified as Type D only when both are elevated together 1.

Negative affectivity is a stable tendency to experience negative emotions across time and situations, including worry, irritability, gloom, anger, and anxiety 1. Social inhibition is the restraint from expressing those feelings and opinions around other people, out of fear of rejection or disapproval 1. Both have held up as relatively stable when measured across a four-year period. So this is not a mood a candidate is having on a Tuesday afternoon.

Johan Denollet, a professor of medical psychology at Tilburg University, described the construct in 1995, building it from clinical observations in cardiac patients 31.

That origin also fixes what the label is not. Denollet has stressed that Type D is not a psychiatric disorder or a marker of psychopathology 1. Someone classified as Type D does not necessarily meet criteria for a clinical condition such as depression 1. Researchers have warned that it gets misread as psychopathology when it was conceived as a normal personality type 2.

Keep one test in mind for any assessment: does it sort people into types, or does it measure what a specific role requires? Type D is the first kind.

The same test applies to other tools that sort people into types, and we run the Enneagram through it in our guide to using it in hiring.

How the DS14 Scores Type D, and Why One Interview Can't

In research, Type D is assigned by a scored questionnaire with fixed numeric cut-offs, not by reading a candidate's demeanor. That instrument is the DS14, and knowing how it works rules out the inferences an interview tempts you to draw.

The DS14 Is a Scored Self-Report Tool

The DS14 is a 14-item questionnaire that produces two separate subscale scores 4. Each item is rated 0 to 4, so each subscale runs from 0 to 28. A person is classified as Type D only when they score 10 or more on both subscales 4.

A Guarded Interview Is Not a Type D Finding

The trouble is that Type D's defining features look almost exactly like ordinary interview nerves. Anxiety, tension, and guardedness in a high-stakes evaluative meeting are what most candidates bring to the room. A related trait, behavioral inhibition, describes withdrawing from unfamiliar situations, and a first-round interview with strangers is unfamiliar for nearly everyone.

What separates a trait from a bad hour is persistence, not intensity. Descriptions of Type D stress that these feelings show up more frequently and more consistently over time than in the general population. Most people feel these things at times.

One conversation cannot establish consistency over time. That does not clear the quiet candidate either. It means you have measured nothing at all. The fix is to make the interview measure job-relevant behavior against a fixed rubric instead.

The Cardiac Studies Type D Came From, and What Broke

Even inside medicine, the label is not on firm ground. Type D was built and tested on heart patients, and the mortality link that made it famous did not survive larger studies. Every foundational study ran in cardiac patient populations, never in workplace or occupational settings.

Those early results looked dramatic. In one cohort of more than 300 cardiac rehabilitation patients, about 27% of Type D patients had died within 10 years, against 6% of non-Type D patients. Among nearly 900 coronary artery bypass patients, Type D patients were reported to be over four times more likely to have a heart attack or die within nine months of surgery.

Then the replications came in. An individual patient data meta-analysis pooling more than 11,000 cardiovascular disease patients across 19 prospective cohort studies found Type D is not a risk factor for mortality in those patients. Researchers concluded that the smaller early studies "may have inadvertently reached exaggerated or false conclusions" 5. A 2023 analysis found smaller samples produced larger Type D effects than larger ones, a pattern "possibly hinting at publication bias" 6.

That argues for more caution, not less, before stretching the label into hiring.

Why Type D Fails as a Candidate Screen

Nothing in the Type D personality research base tests whether someone can do a job. The label was built to predict cardiac events, mortality, and transplant rejection in patients with heart disease 2. It has never been checked against a job-performance, hiring, or personnel-decision criterion.

That matters because the label arrives dressed like a hiring tool. It has a name, a two-letter code, and a defined trait profile. That is exactly the packaging that gets pulled into hiring conversations, whatever the research actually measured.

Where studies do report work-related findings, they are health and distress outcomes. One review reported a negative effect of Type D on work-related problems, including higher absence-leave and higher vital exhaustion 7. Those are markers of strain, studied by health psychologists, not measures of competence.

The clinical findings go further than most managers expect. A systematic review of eight studies found Type D strongly associated with suicidal thoughts and behaviors across populations and countries. That is mental-health territory. A hiring decision is not equipped to screen on it, and is not legally permitted to.

Set the research aside for a moment and the practical mistake is simpler. That first-few-minutes read on a quiet or tense candidate comes first, and the label arrives afterward to explain the hunch. A read that fast is bias in a costume.

Judging on personality profile cuts both ways: you can pass over an outstanding candidate who does not match the expected profile, and advance a weaker one who performed the right mood for the room.

There is also a measurement problem. In a high-stakes selection setting, candidates answer a self-report questionnaire with what they think the employer wants to hear, so the results are not always accurate. Test the work instead.

Where a Health-Linked Label Runs Into the ADA

Type D is riskier to raise in a hiring conversation than ordinary personality-fit talk, because the label was built to flag health risk. Personality itself is not a protected trait, and courts have accepted that an employer can decline to hire over legitimate personality-fit concerns. The protection collapses the moment the trait you are screening works as a stand-in for a perceived impairment.

A Health-Linked Label Can Look Like a Medical Inquiry

Before a job offer, the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) lets you ask only about a candidate's ability to perform job functions, not about the existence, nature, or severity of a condition 8. That ban is broad. It covers any question likely to surface information about a disability, including indirect ones about symptoms, treatment, or general disposition.

The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) draws a clean line for personality-style tools. Tests designed to identify a mental disorder or impairment count as medical examinations; tests measuring traits such as honesty, preferences, and habits do not.

Type D sits on the wrong side of that line. It was never built as a workplace traits instrument. Its whole purpose is flagging cardiovascular and psychological risk, the impairment territory that definition targets.

Courts have found the line crossed without a single clinical word being spoken. In Kroll v. White Lake Ambulance Authority, the Sixth Circuit held that requiring an employee to attend counseling could be a medical examination once the record tied the request to mental health issues. Read that back into your own interview. Calling a candidate guarded or distressed, then acting on it, looks like a response to a perceived psychological profile rather than to job-relevant conduct.

Unvalidated Screens Create ADA and Title VII Problems

The fix is job-relatedness you can show. Courts look for a clear link between the traits a pre-employment test measures and the tasks of the specific job. Type D has no such link. Its validated evidence runs to coronary disease, not to job performance.

Employers using hiring tools are also expected to check whether those tools screen out qualified candidates with disabilities 9. Skipping that check is common. In our own topline report with SHRM Labs, 44.5% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners said nobody formally validates their assessments.

About 76% of organizations with more than 100 employees use some form of personality testing. Widespread testing with nothing checking it is how an ordinary hiring tool turns into a legal problem.

The exposure widens further under the ADA's "regarded as" prong. Since 2008, a candidate is covered if you act against them because of a perceived impairment, with no need to prove any substantial limitation. You will not think of yourself as screening for a health condition. That is the trap, because a label built in health research does it for you.

Behavioral Interview Questions That Score Actions, Not Mood

Replace the impression with a scored process: ask every candidate the same job-relevant questions, and rate each answer against written behavioral anchors. Sackett and colleagues' 2023 meta-analysis places structured interview validity at.42 10. That is the highest-performing standalone predictor among common selection methods, ahead of cognitive tests, work samples, and personality measures 10.

A 2025 meta-analysis by Wingate and colleagues covered 37 studies and 30,646 participants. It splits that prediction by criterion rather than reporting one overall figure: task performance at ρ=.30, and contextual performance, meaning citizenship behavior and team collaboration, at ρ=.28. So the method is validated against job outcomes, not against personality types.

Structure also closes the channel a label like Type D would otherwise flow through. Research published in the Journal of Business and Psychology found standardized formats cut bias effects by more than half compared to freeform conversations, an effect size of d =.23 against d =.59. Behavioral questions do the same work at the question level. They ask what a candidate actually did in a specific past situation, so attention moves from how the person came across to what the person did.

Six steps build the interview:

  1. Pick the competencies the role requires, roughly five core ones, so every question is visibly about the job.
  2. Write 5-7 questions per competency set, phrased as past behavior: what happened, what you did, what resulted.
  3. Write the anchors before the interview, never adjusting them afterward to fit how a candidate performed.
  4. Calibrate the panel in a 30-minute session: walk each question, discuss what a 2 versus a 4 sounds like, and score a mock answer together.
  5. Score independently and write the rating down before anyone speaks in the debrief.
  6. Compare scores against the anchor, not against impressions, when two interviewers land on a 2 and a 4 for the same answer.

Every level of the rubric below describes something observable in the answer, so warmth and delivery have nowhere to enter the score.

Probe a thin or hesitant answer. The one constraint is that a follow-up never changes the rubric the answer is scored against. A guarded first response that opens up under a good follow-up still surfaces the same evidence a talkative candidate volunteered unprompted.

Our topline survey with SHRM Labs found 45.8% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners call their process inconsistent, unsure it picks the best candidate rather than the best interviewer. Writing your score down before the room starts talking is what stops that drift.

Type D Traits on the Job: Fit, Strengths, Risks

Once someone is on your team, you are managing observable behavior, not a diagnosis you are qualified to make. Expect a recognizable cluster: sadness, irritability when work departs from a plan the person laid out, a pessimistic default toward spotting what is wrong, negative self-talk, and avoidance of social situations with people they do not already know.

The same profile carries a second half that managers notice less. They tend to be organized, caring, and thoughtful. Mess in the work environment bothers them, and they are often the last to give up on a task after others have quit.

Fit depends on the work itself. Low-risk work that follows standard, non-negotiable procedures is a strong match, and the stability of routine suits this profile rather than wearing it down. High turnover or an environment that changes regularly is the opposite, since constant change tends to unsettle them.

On a team, they can bring systems and tools that pull disorganized colleagues into better habits, and they often steady a group under pressure. Watch two things. They need to feel useful and appreciated, and because they hold in a lot of emotion, that willingness to serve can be taken advantage of. The other is quieter: in group forums where input is the deliverable, silence reads as nothing to contribute.

Managing Type D Behavior Without Diagnosing It

If a new hire is reserved, quick to expect the worst, or knocked back by small setbacks, the useful question is what is driving it, not what to call it. Start there, then support the person toward more positive thinking, including training if that is what helps them grow in confidence. Steering someone into counseling is not one of those moves: as Kroll above shows, once the record ties that request to mental health it can count as a medical examination. This takes time and patience, and part of the work is reassuring the person of their worth to other people in the organization.

Four moves do most of the work:

  • Match the task to the temperament: the stable, procedural work described above, in a low-turnover team where support is needed and valued, tends to bring out the person's best. Watch the load while you do it, since the willingness to serve noted above is the thing that gets over-drawn.
  • Ask what sits underneath: treat withdrawal or pessimism as a signal about workload, clarity, or confidence, and ask before you interpret.
  • Set short-term goals: they build clarity about personal values and priorities, and teach the person to look ahead with more hope, optimism, and confidence.
  • Change the channel, not the person: if you need input they do not offer in a group, ask in writing or one to one, and use what you get.

It is ordinary management, applied patiently to behavior you can actually see.

Frequently asked questions

What are type A, B, C, and D personalities?

Four popular labels: A for driven and competitive, B for relaxed, C for detail-focused and conflict-avoidant, D for distressed. A and D grew out of cardiac research, while B and C are informal add-ons. None predicts job performance.

Is type D personality the same as introversion?

No. Introversion is a preference for quieter settings and lower stimulation. Type D pairs frequent negative emotion with holding those feelings back around others, out of fear of disapproval 1. A calm introvert is not Type D.

What is the best job for a type D personality?

That is a question about work you already assign, not a filter for your shortlist. Stable, procedural work with low risk and clear standards suits this profile, and routine steadies rather than drains them. Constant change and high turnover are the poor fit. Test the skills, not the type.

What is the difference between type D personality and depression?

Depression is a clinical condition with diagnostic criteria. Type D is a normal personality type, and the researcher who introduced it has said it is not a psychiatric disorder 1. A Type D classification does not mean depression 1.

Can type D personality change over time?

Both traits held up as relatively stable across a four-year period. Behavior still shifts with support: training, short-term goals, and work that suits the person build confidence and a more hopeful outlook.
