# A Skills Assessment Glossary for Recruiters Running Tests

You pick the skills assessment, send it to forty people, and by Friday it has made a decision you now have to defend. The vocabulary that arrived with it was written by psychologists, not recruiters: validity, norm group, cut score, adverse impact.

Getting one of those slightly wrong is expensive. Read a percentile as a percentage and you reject people who cleared the bar you meant to set. Each of these words is a setting you own, and each one changes what the test does before you ever send it.

Quick answer

A skills assessment is a scored test you configure, send, and later have to justify. Match the test type to what the role actually needs, and place it right after the application so the scores build your shortlist instead of trimming one a recruiter already made. Ask a vendor which validity study covers your job, not whether one exists. Set your cut score from your own pool size and your own performance data, then check the pass rates it produces group by group before you launch it on anybody.

What a Skills Assessment Is, and Why Companies Run One

A skills assessment is a test you send before an offer that measures whether a candidate can do the job, scored the same way for everyone who sits it 1. That scoring rule is what separates it from a quiz stapled to a job post. Same questions, same time, same line between pass and fail, for the person you liked on the phone and the person you did not.

Think about what it replaces. A recruiter reads eighty resumes on a Tuesday, forms an impression of each one in about nine seconds, and never writes down what the impression rested on. That is judgment nobody can audit. A scored test at least leaves a number you can point at, argue with, and check later against how the hire actually worked out.

The business case is usually retention. A 2023 Society for Human Resource Management study found that organizations using validated pre-employment assessments cut turnover by an average of 65% compared with those relying on resumes and interviews alone 2. That matters most in volume hiring, where one weak screening habit quietly repeats itself across every hire you make this quarter. The trade is that you now own a selection decision somebody can question. The vendor built the test; you chose it, placed it, and set the line.

Types of Skills Assessment Tests, and How to Choose One

Five kinds of test carry predictive power in hiring: cognitive ability, skills or job knowledge, personality, situational judgment, and work samples 3. They are not interchangeable, because each one catches a different way a hire goes wrong.

Test typeWhat it measuresWhere it fits
Cognitive abilityHow fast someone learns and works through unfamiliar problemsTrainees, early-career hires, any role where the work keeps changing
Skills or job knowledgeOne specific technical ability: coding, Excel, writing, typingDefined-skill roles such as support, data entry, technical delivery
Work samplePerformance on a real deliverable from the actual jobSenior and specialist roles you cannot judge from a resume
Situational judgmentWhat someone does inside a realistic workplace scenarioCall centre, retail, hospitality, first-line management
PersonalityWork style, reliability, service orientationFrontline roles where turnover is the thing you are fighting

Pick two or three for a role rather than running the set. If you can only add one, make it cognitive ability, which predicts across the widest range of roles 3. Start by writing down the two or three things without which a new hire fails in their first ninety days, then choose the test that measures those and ignore the rest of the catalogue.

Where the test sits, and the forty-minute ceiling

Placement changes the value of a test as much as the choice of it. Put the assessment at step two, straight after the application and before anyone reads a resume, so the scores build your shortlist instead of thinning one a recruiter already spent a morning assembling 4. Personality instruments are the single exception, and the section on medical examinations explains why they have to wait.

Placement also decides who never finishes. Criteria looked at roughly half a million tests and found completion holds around 80% while the assessment stays under 40 minutes, then falls to about 60% once total testing runs past an hour 5. Read that as a cost rather than a statistic. The candidate who abandons your test at minute fifty-two is not a weak candidate. She is a senior engineer who already has a job and no particular need for yours, and you removed her from your own pipeline.

So treat forty minutes as a ceiling for the screening stage, not a target to fill. If the role needs more depth, run a short screen first and a longer assessment after the shortlist, so the demanding one is only sat by people you would be glad to hire.

Validity: Whether the Test Predicts Anything at All

Validity is the word a vendor makes its central claim in, and it does not mean quite what it sounds like. A test is never validated in the abstract. What gets validated is the inference you draw when you screen somebody out at a particular score, which is why the claim has to be checked against your job rather than accepted in general 6.

Four kinds get quoted, and they are worth very different amounts. Face validity means the test looks job-related to whoever is looking at it, which is about candidate goodwill and nothing else. Content validity means subject matter experts confirmed the questions cover what the job actually demands. Construct validity means the test measures the trait it names, rather than familiarity with the format. Criterion validity means somebody checked whether higher scorers went on to perform better, and it is the only one of the four that answers the question you are really asking.

When you get to a number, read it against the right yardstick. Anything above roughly 0.30 supports using a test to predict how future candidates will perform 7. That looks feeble next to the correlations you meet in other contexts. Job performance is a messy outcome with a hundred causes, and a 0.30 relationship moves real money once you are hiring at volume. A vendor quoting 0.33 is being straight with you. A vendor quoting 0.85 is describing something other than performance on your job.

Validated for whose job? The call centre problem

Unless a test has been validated for your specific job, it is not valid for that job 8. Keep that sentence to hand when a vendor emails you a study, because evidence collected on another company's workforce in another industry usually says nothing about yours.

Job titles are what make this easy to get wrong. Two companies both hire call centre agents. In the first, the work is inbound: taking calls, answering questions, pulling up an account while somebody waits on the line 8. In the second, it is outbound sales, and the job is persistence, recovering from a hang-up, and holding an unscripted conversation with a stranger who did not ask to be called. Same title on the job board, two completely different jobs. A test that sorts the first group well can sort the second group at close to random.

So the useful request is not "send me your validation evidence" but "show me the study on a job that looks like mine, and tell me who decided it looks like mine." There is a formal route for borrowing evidence between employers, and it still requires a proper analysis of your job before the borrowed numbers mean anything. A vendor who talks about the borrowing and skips the analysis has told you which half of the work they did.

Reliability, and Why a Score Is a Range Rather Than a Number

Reliability asks a simpler question than validity: if the same person sat this test again next month, would they land on roughly the same score? If the answer is no, nothing else about the test matters, because you are not measuring a person, you are measuring a mood.

Vendors report it as a coefficient topping out at 1.00. There is a conventional minimum below which the estimate is too weak to hang a hiring decision on, and what you want is a number sitting comfortably clear of it rather than scraping past 9. Ask which kind of consistency the number describes, too. Whether scores hold steady over time and whether the questions inside one test agree with each other are two separate claims, and they get quoted interchangeably.

The practical consequence is that no score is a fact. Every score is an estimate with a band around it, and the width of that band has a name, the standard error of measurement 10. A 68 and a 72 can be the same candidate on two different mornings. So wherever you draw your line, the people sitting within a few points of it were sorted partly by chance. Know that before a hiring manager asks you why their favourite candidate was cut.

Norm Groups: Why the Same Score Lands at Two Different Percentiles

A raw score is the count of questions somebody got right. A percentile is a position: it tells you what share of a comparison group scored at or below that person. Norming is the conversion between the two, and the raw score itself never shows up in what you see on screen.

That comparison group is the norm group, and the vendor chose it, not you. It might be the general working population, or graduates, or people already doing the job well. The same answers land in different places depending on which one was used. A raw score of 28 out of 40 sits at the 80th percentile against general population norms and at the 55th against graduate norms 11. Nothing about the candidate changed between those two readings.

The expensive mistake is reading a percentile as a percentage. On a hard test, someone at the 70th percentile may have answered fewer than 60% of the questions correctly 12. A recruiter sees 70, decides that is a soft pass, and rejects a candidate who outscored seven people in ten. So the question to ask about any percentile is never what did they score. It is scored against whom.

Cut Score: How to Set the Line You Screen On

The cut score is the threshold that splits pass from fail, and people decide it. There is no natural line inside a test between competent and not competent 13. Somebody chooses where it goes, and if that somebody was not you, it was whatever the platform shipped with.

Two habits do most of the damage. The first is the round number left over from school: 70% passes, below that fails. That convention is not legally defensible and does not meet international testing standards, largely because it takes no account of how hard the questions were 14. The second is one number applied to every role, which over-screens your entry-level jobs and under-screens your senior ones at the same time.

Work from volume instead of from the score. If you need ten hires and expect eighty applicants, a cut at the 90th percentile passes about eight people before anybody drops out, takes another offer, or fails an interview. The maths has already failed and the first application has not arrived.

Then calibrate against your own staff. Pull ninety-day performance data on people already doing the job and find where the strong ones cluster. If they sit between the 72nd and 88th percentile, your own evidence points at a line near the 70th, not the 75th somebody typed into a policy document three years ago 12.

Adverse Impact and the Four-Fifths Rule

Adverse impact is what happens when one selection standard, applied identically to everyone, produces very different pass rates across demographic groups. It is a property of your results, not of anybody's intent. Which is why you measure it on your own hiring data rather than judging it by reading the test.

The four-fifths rule is the arithmetic that flags it. It comes from the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures, and it holds that a selection rate for any race, sex or ethnic group falling below four-fifths of the rate for the highest group will generally be treated by federal enforcement agencies as evidence of adverse impact 15.

Run it on your last hiring round. Say you assessed 50 women and hired 30, a 60% rate, and assessed 100 men and hired 80, an 80% rate. Divide 60 by 80 and you get 75%, under the four-fifths threshold, so the rule is violated 16. That is a prompt rather than a verdict. The 80% figure is a rule of thumb, not a legal definition, and the question underneath it is always whether the test is job-related and necessary for the business.

Your cut score is the lever on this ratio. The higher you set the line, the wider the gap between groups tends to open 17. So before you commit to a threshold, ask the provider for pass rates broken down by group at that exact score. A provider who cannot produce them has answered a different question.

Accommodations, and When a Test Is a Pre-Employment Medical Exam

A reasonable accommodation is a change to how the test is normally run that lets a candidate with a disability show what they can actually do 18. The Americans with Disabilities Act asks you to run the assessment so the score reflects the skill you are measuring rather than the impairment sitting in front of it.

Most requests are unremarkable. Extra time on a timed online test. Questions read aloud where a screen reader will not talk to your platform. Interview questions sent ahead with written answers allowed. What catches employers out is failing to notice a request was made at all, not the cost of any of that. The candidate never has to say the words disability, accommodation, or ADA 19. "I need more time to finish the online assessment, is there a way to extend it?" is a request, and answering it as a scheduling query is exactly where the exposure starts.

When a personality test counts as a medical examination

Before you make an offer, the ADA bars disability-related inquiries and medical examinations outright. The word medical reaches further than most recruiters expect. It covers any procedure seeking information about somebody's physical or mental impairments or health, and personality tests are named directly among the things you cannot require pre-offer 20.

The case that settled it is Karraker v. Rent-A-Center. The company put management candidates through the MMPI, deliberately used the vendor's vocational scoring rather than the clinical version, and had no psychologist read the results. The court held that none of that saved them. However you choose to score the instrument, it can disadvantage candidates with mental disorders, so it is best treated as a medical examination 21.

The rule that falls out of this is easy to operate. An instrument that could surface a mental health condition waits until you have made a conditional offer. At that point you may run it, as long as every candidate entering that job category takes the same one 22. That is why personality sits apart from the other four test types in the table above. It cannot live at step two with the rest, however much you want the scores early.

Frequently asked questions

What is involved in a skills assessment?

Three things: a short timed test of the skills the role needs, scoring against a comparison group, and a pass mark you decide before anyone sits it. Keep the whole thing short, because long tests lose good candidates.

Can you give me an example of a skills assessment?

A ten-minute Excel task where a support candidate cleans and sorts a real spreadsheet, scored automatically. A coding exercise, a typing test, or a written reply to an angry customer all count too.

What is the difference between a skills assessment and an aptitude test?

A skills test asks what somebody can already do today. An aptitude test asks how quickly they will pick up something new. Use aptitude for trainees, skills tests for roles with a defined toolset.

Can a candidate retake a skills assessment they failed?

Only if your retake rule is written down and applied to everybody the same way. Set a waiting period and use a different set of questions, because a second run at the same ones measures memory.

Do I have to tell candidates the assessment is being recorded?

Yes. Tell them before they start what is watched, what is stored, and for how long. Some places demand more: Illinois regulates biometric data, and New York City requires notice for automated hiring tools.
