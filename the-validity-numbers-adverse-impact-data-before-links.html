# The Disparate Impact Numbers Behind Every Hiring Test

A hiring test can predict job performance well and still get a company sued. That happens when it turns down one protected group far more than another, a pattern the EEOC calls disparate impact. Cognitive tests [tests of reasoning and problem-solving] predict job performance better than any other method, and carry the widest race gap of any hiring test.

A 2025 order pulled back federal enforcement, but the law itself did not change. Lawsuits can still go ahead. This piece lines up the real numbers for cognitive, personality, and situational judgment tests (SJTs) next to the rules that judge them. That lets you defend a specific test choice with real numbers, not just a general claim of fairness.

Quick answer

Disparate impact is when a hiring step rejects one protected group far more than others. The EEOC's four-fifths rule is the standard test for it, but courts don't always treat that ratio as the final word. Cognitive tests predict job performance better than any other method. They also carry the widest race gap of any test type.

Personality tests and SJTs predict more modestly, but produce smaller gaps between groups. Score banding, mixed test batteries, and video formats all narrow the cognitive-test gap without lowering its predictive power. The strongest defense mixes test types and writes down the reasoning behind every cutoff score.

What Is Disparate Impact? The Four-Fifths Rule

The EEOC (Equal Employment Opportunity Commission) calls the four-fifths rule a rule of thumb, not a legal standard 1. Say a hiring tool picks 50% of male candidates but only 33% of female candidates. That ratio falls below 80%, so the rule flags it as adverse impact 2.

That calculation is just a screening check. It is not a legal finding of guilt or bias, and it only invites more scrutiny 3.

How Well Cognitive Tests Predict Job Performance

Schmidt and Hunter's 1998 study covered 85 years of hiring research. It found a link of 0.51 between general mental ability (GMA) and job performance, the strongest predictor of any method studied 4.

That link isn't fixed. It rises with how complex the job is. Hunter and Hunter (1984) found 0.56 for complex jobs, 0.51 for mid-level jobs, and 0.40 for simple jobs 4. Later work found this holds true across most jobs 5.

A 2022 review by Sackett and colleagues re-checked the numbers using stricter methods. The score for general mental ability fell from 0.51 to 0.31 4. The ranking held: both studies still put general mental ability first 6. That 0.31 figure is the safer one to use, and it's the baseline this piece works from.

Why Cognitive Tests Carry the Widest Adverse-Impact Gap

Cognitive tests produce the largest adverse impact gap of any test type. Roth and colleagues (2001) found a gap of d = 1.10 [a big difference] between Black and White test takers on cognitive tests 4. DeShon (1998) found the same pattern. Why it happens is still debated 7.

Te Nijenhuis and colleagues (2024) found the same gap in 2020s UK data on high-stakes tests of mental skill 8. Woods (2024) says cognitive testing still works against some racial and ethnic groups more than others, in many settings 9.

Cognitive test gaps between men and women are tiny, from d = 0.00 to 0.10 4. This is about race, not gender.

Three Ways to Shrink the Gap Without Losing Accuracy

A fixed cutoff score on a cognitive test tends to trip the four-fifths rule, given gaps this size 4. Three fixes narrow the gap without dropping the test:

  • Score bands: treat scores within a range as tied, instead of ranking every point 4.
  • Mixed test batteries: pair the cognitive test with a structured interview, a work sample, or a check on conscientiousness [being organized and careful]. One test type alone raises the odds of screening out a group. A mix lets people show strength more than one way 10.
  • Easier formats: picture-based and non-verbal reasoning tests show a smaller gap than word-heavy ones: d = 1.02 versus 1.08 4. Non-verbal formats often show smaller gaps than standard cognitive tests 10.

None of these close the gap completely. Each one shrinks it while keeping the predictive power from the section above.

Personality Tests Predict Performance, Modestly

Personality [trait] tests predict job performance, but only modestly. Conscientiousness is the trait most tied to performance, scoring r =.31, in the usual 0.20–0.30 range 411. That link adds to cognitive testing. It does not replace it 4.

Which personality model you use also matters. The Big Five [a five-trait model] has strong backing from research for hiring 11. The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI), which sorts people into 16 fixed types, does not 11. Framing questions around real work moments lifts the score further — enough that Sackett, Zhang, Berry, and Lievens (2022) call it its own test type 12.

Paired with GMA, a combined conscientiousness score reaches R =.60 4. Even so, evidence for personality tests used alone stays mixed.

Adverse Impact in Personality Tests, and the ADA Risk

Personality tests carry a different adverse impact risk than cognitive tests. The risk comes from which traits get tested and the selection ratio [how many people get through], not a race gap in raw scores. Advance the top scorers, and a fourth applicant loses out over a tiny gap, as Hausdorf and Risavy found (2010, 2011) 10.

One study found racial gaps in faking only showed up when applicants had a real reason to fake their answers. Gender gaps on tests with no such incentive moved the other way: they shrank instead of grew 13.

Personality tests carry a separate risk under the ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act). A test that asks about mood or worry can act like a medical exam and screen out disabled people 11. Employers can't ask applicants if they're disabled, but vendors can study this and should check if their tests screen out this group.

Vendor systems that compare applicants to current top performers can make this worse, since disabled people often make up fewer of them. Employers can still use a test with this effect if it's needed for the job and the business. The better fix isn't defending the test itself: disabled applicants can ask for a different test, or opt out, as a required adjustment [accommodation] 14.

Situational Judgment Tests: What They Predict, and Where They Stop Adding Value

SJTs predict job performance at roughly.26 to.34 15. They give you a workplace scenario and ask how you'd respond. McDaniel, Morgeson, Finnegan, Campion, and Braverman's 2001 estimate of about.34 is the most cited figure in the field 15.

A later study by Christian, Edwards, and Bradley found the score depends on what the SJT measures 15. Tests aimed at leadership and people skills land at the higher end of that range 15.

SJTs add only a little on top of tests you already use. Beyond cognitive tests alone, they add roughly.03 to.08, depending on the study and how much reasoning the SJT demands 15. The gain over personality tests is similarly small 15.

How you build an SJT changes its score almost as much as what it measures. Ask "what's the best response?" and it leans more on cognitive skill 15. Ask "what would you actually do?" and it leans more on personality traits 15. Scoring matters too: keys built from real outcome data predict roughly twice as well as keys built from expert opinion alone 16.

When SJTs Lose Their Adverse-Impact Advantage

SJTs show a Black-White gap of about d = 0.38 [in between small and medium], roughly a third the size of cognitive tests' 1.02–1.08 range 15174. But that number only holds under certain conditions.

Test-Design Choices That Shrink or Widen the SJT Gap

Format decisions move the gap as much as the sample does. Fixed-choice formats let you pick from a set list of answers. They score faster, but they widen the gap between groups and invite more faking than open-ended formats 2015.

A scoring key built around one "correct" answer acts more like a cognitive test and widens the gap 15. A key built around normal work behavior lowers the reasoning demand and narrows it 15. Video SJTs produce a smaller gap than written ones, even though video shows raters a candidate's race 20.

Disparate Impact Liability Survives the Executive Order

Disparate-impact law traces to Griggs v. Duke Power Co. (1971), the case that set the standard — and now reaches software, too 22. The EEOC launched an effort in 2021 to hold AI and machine-learning hiring tools to the same civil rights laws 23, and by May 2023 confirmed the four-fifths rule applies to AI-driven hiring under Title VII 2324.

The EEOC got more than 88,000 bias complaints in fiscal year 2024 — a third straight yearly rise 25. Its 2025 report logged a 42% jump in AI-related probes, with settlements reaching into the millions 26. Then that posture reversed: Executive Order 14281 told federal agencies to pull back on disparate-impact enforcement, and the EEOC stopped probing disparate-impact-only charges 27. The agency also pulled its AI guidance in 2025 28.

None of this erases the underlying risk. Disparate impact is still part of Title VII, and private lawsuits over it can go ahead no matter what the EEOC does 29. A company whose hiring process creates an unfair gap between groups can still face a lawsuit, even with fewer federal probes 29.

If you're defending a specific test choice, the real question isn't whether the law changed. It's whether the evidence behind that choice would hold up in court, even without the EEOC asking first.

What Does This Mean for Employers? What Should They Do to Avoid Litigation?

None of this means cognitive tests are off the table. They still predict best of any single method. It means not leaning on one fixed cutoff alone. Mix test types, score in bands, use easier formats, and write down why you set each cutoff. If a test touches mood or health, offer disabled candidates another path or an opt-out.

Frequently asked questions

Is disparate impact still illegal?

Yes. It's still part of Title VII, even after a 2025 order pulled EEOC enforcement back. Private lawsuits over it can still go ahead 29.

What is the 80% rule for disparate impact?

The EEOC's four-fifths rule flags adverse impact when one group's pass rate falls below 80% of another's. It's a screening flag, not a legal finding 12.

What is an example of disparate impact?

Say a hiring tool picks 50% of male candidates but only 33% of female ones. That falls below the EEOC's 80% test, flagging adverse impact 2.

What is the difference between disparate impact and disparate treatment?

Disparate treatment is picking on one person on purpose because of a protected trait. Disparate impact is a neutral rule that ends up hurting one protected group more than others.
