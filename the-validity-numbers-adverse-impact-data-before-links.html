# Validity Numbers and Adverse Impact of Each Hiring Test

You are about to buy a test. The vendor sends over its validity figure. One number, two decimal places, and it looks like the answer. Ask the same vendor what the pass rates look like broken out by race, and the conversation usually slows down.

Disparate impact is what sits in that silence: a test that looks neutral on its face but rejects one protected group at a far higher rate than another. Every method you can buy carries two numbers. One is a validity coefficient, the other a score gap. The two tend to rise together. What follows pairs them, method by method, so you can see the trade you are making before you sign anything.

TL;DR

  • The selection methods that predict job performance best also produce the widest racial score gaps, so every assessment decision is a trade-off you should be able to defend with evidence on paper.
  • Dropping the test in favor of an unstructured interview does not remove the gap. It removes your ability to see it.
  • A group selection rate below 80% of the highest group's rate flags adverse impact and shifts the burden onto you to prove the test is job-related.
  • Executive Order 14281 paused federal enforcement, not the law itself, so private plaintiffs and city regulators still hold you to the same arithmetic. Mixed batteries, banded scores, and cutoffs set in writing remain the practical defense.

Why Higher Test Validity Often Means Bigger Score Gaps

Rank the common selection methods by how well they predict job performance. Now rank them again by the gap between Black and White candidate scores. The two lists look uncomfortably similar. Cognitive ability tests sit near the top of both. That is the trade at the center of every assessment decision you make. No vendor puts both numbers on the same page for you.

Read the validity column with its date attached. A reanalysis published in 2022 pulled most of these coefficients down, so a figure quoted without a vintage is close to meaningless. Read the gap column with a different kind of caution. It is expressed as d, the distance between two group averages measured in standard deviations. A d of 1.10 means the average Black candidate scores more than a full standard deviation below the average White candidate. At any fixed cutoff, that gap is enough to move pass rates into audit territory.

The situational judgment test row shows why the source of a gap figure matters as much as its size. Measured on people who already hold the job, the difference is 0.38. That sounds manageable. Measured on the applicants who actually sit the test, it is 0.66. You run your pass-rate calculation on applicants, not incumbents. When a vendor hands you a comfortable number, ask who was in the sample before you rely on it.

The bottom row is the one to sit with. Dropping the test and trusting a conversation instead makes the problem worse rather than better. Huffcutt and Roth found that unstructured interviews produce group differences comparable to, and in some studies larger than, cognitive ability tests 8. Almost nobody audits interview pass rates the way they audit test scores 8. In our own survey with SHRM, 45.8% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners described their process as inconsistent, varying by hiring manager. An employer who swaps a validated test for hiring-manager judgment can increase real discrimination while shrinking the evidence of it.

Why the 2022 Sackett Reanalysis Cut Validity Coefficients

Two credible sources will quote you different validity coefficients for the same assessment method, and one of them is not wrong. The 2022 Sackett reanalysis is usually the reason. Knowing which side of it a number sits on matters more than the number itself.

Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens went back through the meta-analytic evidence behind the rankings the field had leaned on since 1998. They found a flaw in how earlier researchers corrected for range restriction. Range restriction is the narrowing that happens when a study can only look at the people who were hired rather than everyone who applied. The correction had been too generous. It inflated validity estimates by an average of.10 to.20 across the board 9.

The error was mechanical rather than ideological. That correction formula assumes a study tested live applicants and then hired on the very score being validated. Most of the underlying research did nothing of the kind. It tested people already doing the job, where the restriction is indirect and much smaller 2. Researchers applied an applicant-sized correction to incumbent data. That pushed every coefficient upward, method by method, for two decades.

The ordering survived the correction. Methods that ranked high in 1998 still rank high. Only the absolute numbers shrank 9. So a vendor quoting.51 for cognitive ability is not lying to you, it is quoting a pre-2022 figure. Avoid putting the two vintages side by side in the same comparison, because that quietly makes the older method look stronger. Ask which analysis a coefficient comes from before you let it settle an argument.

Cognitive Ability Tests: High Validity, High Adverse Impact

Cognitive ability tests are the sharpest tool covered here, and they are also the most likely to cut you. They predict performance well. They are cheap to run at scale. They also carry the largest and most consistently documented adverse impact of any common assessment format.

How much they help depends on the job. Hunter and Hunter reported a validity of.56 for high-complexity roles against.40 for low-complexity work 3. In practice that means a general mental ability test earns its place on an actuarial analyst opening. It barely earns that place on a warehouse role, where the same test still produces the same score gap. If the complexity is not there, you are buying the adverse impact and leaving most of the prediction on the table.

None of this is a historical problem that better test design has since solved. Woods reviewed the evidence in 2024 and concluded that cognitive testing continues to show a systematic adverse impact against people from particular minority ethnic backgrounds in many settings 10. Assume the gap will appear in your own data, because it very likely will.

That points to a use rule rather than a ban. Run a cognitive test where the role demands the reasoning it measures. Never make it the single gate that decides who advances. Check the resulting pass rates by group every quarter, rather than at the end of the hiring season. The test is not the liability. An unexamined cutoff sitting on top of it is.

What the Four-Fifths Rule Flags and What You Must Prove

The four-fifths rule turns a score gap into arithmetic anyone in the room can follow. Take each group's selection rate. Divide it by the rate of the group that did best, then look at the answer. Anything under 80% flags adverse impact 11. If a tool advances 10% of male candidates and only 7% of female candidates, the ratio is 70%, and you have a flag 12.

The arithmetic misfires when the numbers are small. With a handful of candidates in either group, two or three individual decisions can push the ratio above or below 80% by chance alone. That is why a statistical significance test runs alongside it 12. An employer sitting at a 78% ratio on twelve hires is in a very different position from one sitting at 50% on four hundred.

What the rule is not matters too. The EEOC treats it as a rule of thumb rather than a bright-line legal standard 13. A ratio under 80% is not a finding of guilt. A ratio over 80% is not a clean bill of health. It is a smoke alarm. It decides who has to start explaining.

What the employer has to prove once the ratio flags

An adverse impact flag does not make your test unlawful. It moves the burden. From that point you have to demonstrate that the procedure is job-related and consistent with business necessity. In practice that means producing validation evidence that the abilities the test measures are required to do the work 12. If that evidence does not exist yet, no one can assemble it with any credibility after a charge is filed.

The case record shows what happens when it is missing. In U.S. v. City of New York, the Department of Justice and the EEOC sued over the fire department's written entrance exams. The exams disproportionately excluded Black and Hispanic candidates. The courts found they lacked content validity, meaning nobody could show that what the exam asked mirrored what the job required 14. The exam was not struck down for being hard. It was struck down for being unconnected to firefighting.

That is the practical lesson for anyone buying a test today. A narrow, job-specific assessment built from an analysis of the role can be defended sentence by sentence, because each item points at a task somebody actually performs. A general-purpose test bought off a shelf and run across every opening cannot. Before you buy, ask the vendor for the technical manual and check whether the validation study covers roles that look like yours.

Executive Order 14281 Did Not Repeal Disparate Impact

In April 2025, Executive Order 14281 directed federal agencies to deprioritize disparate-impact enforcement. The EEOC stopped investigating charges that rest on that theory alone. It also pulled its algorithmic hiring guidance from its website 13. If your read of that was that the exposure has gone away, the next two paragraphs are the important ones.

An executive order sets agency priorities. It cannot amend a statute. Disparate impact is written into Title VII at 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(k) 12. Private plaintiffs can still bring disparate-impact claims in court, whatever the agency chooses to pursue 13. The liability did not disappear. What changed is the party most likely to raise it, which is now a law firm rather than an investigator. Law firms do not send warning letters first.

Day-to-day accountability has also moved down to state and city law. Those obligations are specific and already in force. New York City's Local Law 144 requires you to run an annual independent bias audit of an automated hiring tool. You must publish a summary of the results and give candidates notice at least 10 business days before you use it 12. California's Civil Rights Council regulations go further. They extend disparate-impact liability for automated decision systems to vendors as well as employers 12.

Because these frameworks lean on the same arithmetic described above, the 80% threshold now works as a practical compliance floor for any employer hiring across several states. You cannot run one policy in New York and a looser one everywhere else without keeping two sets of records. Most teams therefore hold every requisition to the strictest rule they face.

How to Reduce Adverse Impact Without Losing Validity

Meeting that floor comes down to decisions you already control. Adverse impact is a property of how a test is used, not a fixed flaw baked into cognitive measurement. The single biggest lever is the cutoff. A rigid pass mark on a cognitive test is what turns a group score difference into a four-fifths violation 3. Move the mark two points and the ratio moves with it. That is exactly why you have to set the mark on evidence, and set it before anyone applies.

The composition of the battery matters just as much. Sackett and colleagues put cognitive scores alongside structured interviews, work samples, or a conscientiousness measure. Those combinations produced smaller group differences than the cognitive test alone, and prediction of job performance held up in the multi-method systems 8. You are not trading accuracy for fairness here. You are trading a single narrow signal for several. An experienced hiring panel does that anyway when it is working well.

None of it protects you without a record behind it. A defensible file holds three things:

  • Validation evidence tying scores to performance in the role you are actually hiring for, backed by a technical manual the vendor can produce on request.
  • Cutoffs recorded in writing before the first candidate is seen, banded rather than scored point by point, with the reasoning documented alongside them.
  • Quarterly pass-rate checks against the 80% threshold, run as a standing habit rather than a one-off audit commissioned after somebody complains.

That standard is still rare. In our survey with SHRM Labs, 44.5% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners said nobody formally validates their assessments. A battery you cannot validate is a battery you cannot defend, and the moment to find that out is not the moment a charge arrives.

Frequently asked questions

What is an example of disparate impact?

Every applicant takes a written exam, and Black candidates pass at well under 80% of the White rate. The rule is neutral on its face and the outcome is not. Griggs v. Duke Power turned on exactly that 14.

What is the difference between disparate impact and disparate treatment?

Disparate treatment is intentional, a decision aimed at a protected group. Disparate impact needs no intent at all. Unequal pass rates from a neutral practice are enough, and you then have to prove business necessity 12.

How many candidates do you need for an adverse impact analysis?

There is no legal minimum. The ratio gets unreliable below roughly 30 selections per group. With fewer than that, lean on a statistical significance test rather than the 80% figure by itself 12.

Is the vendor or the employer liable for a biased hiring test?

Under Title VII, the employer is liable, even when a vendor built the test. California now extends that liability to vendors as well 12. Ask for the validation evidence before you buy, not after.
