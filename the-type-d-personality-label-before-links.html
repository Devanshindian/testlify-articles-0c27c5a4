# Type D Personality: What the Research Supports for Hiring

A candidate is quiet, tense, hard to read. Someone in the debrief puts a name to it: Type D personality. The label sounds like a hiring signal, and about 76% of organizations with more than 100 employees already run some form of personality testing.

Type D came out of heart-disease research. It has never been tested against job performance. Judge a candidate on that read and you can lose a strong hire to a smoother talker.

The Americans with Disabilities Act also covers a candidate you treat as impaired, whether or not they are. What holds up is duller and better. Ask every candidate the same job-relevant questions, score them against written standards, then manage what you actually see.

What Type D Personality Means: Distressed, Not Disordered

Type D personality is a term from medical psychology, not a workplace category, and the D stands for distressed 12. It describes two traits turning up together: negative affectivity and social inhibition 1. You are only Type D when both run high 1.

Negative affectivity is a steady pull toward negative feeling, whatever the day brings: worry, irritability, gloom, anger, anxiety 1. Social inhibition is holding those feelings and opinions back around other people, for fear of being rejected or judged 1. Both stayed fairly stable when researchers measured them over four years. So this is not a mood a candidate is having on a Tuesday afternoon.

Johan Denollet, a professor of medical psychology at Tilburg University, named it in 1995, building it from what he saw in heart patients 31.

That origin also fixes what the label is not. Denollet has been clear that Type D is not a psychiatric disorder or a sign of mental illness 1. Someone classified as Type D need not meet the criteria for a condition such as depression 1. Researchers have warned that people misread it as mental illness, when it was meant to describe a normal personality type 2.

Keep one test in mind for any assessment. Does it sort people into types, or does it measure what the job actually requires? Type D is the first kind.

How the DS14 Scores Type D, and Why One Interview Can't

In research, nobody assigns Type D by reading a candidate's face. It comes from a scored questionnaire with fixed number cut-offs. That questionnaire is the DS14, and once you see how it works, the guesses an interview invites fall apart.

The DS14 Is a Scored Self-Report Tool

The DS14 asks 14 questions and produces two separate scores 4. Each answer is rated 0 to 4, so each score runs from 0 to 28 5. A person is classified Type D only when they score 10 or more on both 4.

A Guarded Interview Is Not a Type D Finding

Type D's defining features look almost exactly like ordinary interview nerves. Anxiety, tension and guardedness are what most candidates bring to a high-stakes meeting.

A related trait, behavioral inhibition, means pulling back from unfamiliar situations 6. A first-round interview with strangers is unfamiliar for nearly everyone.

What separates a trait from a bad hour is how often it shows up, not how strong it looks. Descriptions of Type D stress that these feelings recur, over time, far more than in most people 6. Most of us feel all of it at some point 6.

One conversation cannot show that anything recurs. So the honest verdict on a quiet candidate is not that they are probably fine. You measured nothing at all. Fix that by making the interview score job-relevant behavior against a fixed rubric.

The Heart-Disease Studies Type D Came From, and What Broke

Even inside medicine, the label is shaky. Type D was built and tested on heart patients, and the death-risk link that made it famous did not survive bigger studies. Every foundational study ran in cardiac patient groups, never in a workplace.

The early results looked dramatic. One study followed more than 300 cardiac rehabilitation patients. About 27% of the Type D patients had died within 10 years, against 6% of the rest 6.

Another followed nearly 900 heart bypass patients for nine months. Type D patients were reported to be over four times more likely to have a heart attack or die 6.

Then other teams tried to repeat it. A pooled analysis of more than 11,000 cardiovascular disease patients across 19 studies found Type D is not a risk factor for death 7. The researchers concluded that the smaller early studies "may have inadvertently reached exaggerated or false conclusions" 8. A 2023 analysis found smaller samples produced bigger Type D effects than larger ones, a pattern "possibly hinting at publication bias" 9.

That argues for more caution before stretching the label into hiring, not less.

Why Type D Fails as a Candidate Screen

Nothing in the Type D research tests whether someone can do a job. The label was built to predict heart attacks, death and transplant rejection in people who already have heart disease 2. It has never been checked against job performance, hiring, or any personnel decision.

It matters because the label arrives dressed like a hiring tool. It has a name, a two-letter code, and a tidy trait profile. That packaging is what pulls it into hiring conversations, whatever the research actually measured.

Where studies do look at work, they measure health and strain. One review reported a negative effect of Type D on work-related problems, including more absence leave and more exhaustion 10. Those are signs of strain, studied by health psychologists. They are not measures of competence.

The clinical findings go further than most managers expect. A review of eight studies found Type D strongly linked to suicidal thoughts and behavior across countries and populations. That is mental-health territory. A hiring decision cannot screen for it, and legally it may not.

Put the research aside and the practical mistake is simpler. Managers rarely reject someone for being Type D. They reject a quiet or guarded candidate on gut feel in the first few minutes, then reach for a label that dresses up the hunch. A read that fast is bias in a costume.

Judging on personality cuts both ways. You can pass over an outstanding candidate who does not match the profile you expected, and hire a weaker one who performed the right mood for the room 11.

There is a measurement problem too. In a high-stakes hiring process, candidates answer a self-report questionnaire with what they think you want to hear, so the results are not always accurate 12. Test the work instead.

Where a Health-Linked Label Runs Into the ADA

Type D is riskier to raise than ordinary talk about fit, because the label was built to flag health risk. Personality itself is not protected, and courts have accepted that an employer can decline to hire over personality-fit concerns 13. That protection collapses the moment the trait you screen on stands in for an impairment you think you see.

A Health-Linked Label Can Count as a Medical Question

Before you make an offer, the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) limits what you can ask. You can ask whether a candidate is able to do the job. You cannot ask whether they have a condition, or how bad it is 1415.

That ban is broad. It covers any question likely to turn up a disability, including sideways ones about symptoms, treatment, or general disposition 15.

The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) draws a clean line for personality-style tools. A test designed to spot a mental disorder or impairment counts as a medical examination. A test measuring honesty, preferences and habits does not 16.

Type D sits on the wrong side of that line. It was never built to measure traits at work. Its whole purpose is flagging heart and psychological risk, the impairment territory that definition targets 17.

Courts have found the line crossed with no clinical word spoken. In Kroll v. White Lake Ambulance Authority, the Sixth Circuit looked at an employer who required an employee to attend counseling. Once the record tied that demand to mental health, it could count as a medical examination 16.

Read that back into your own interview. Call a candidate guarded or distressed, then act on it, and you are responding to a profile you assumed, not to their conduct 16.

A Screen Nobody Checked Creates ADA and Title VII Problems

The fix is job-relatedness you can show. Courts look for a clear link between what a pre-employment test measures and the tasks of the job 18. Type D has no such link. Its evidence runs to coronary disease, not job performance.

You are also expected to check whether your hiring tools screen out qualified candidates with disabilities 19. Plenty of employers skip it. In our own topline report with SHRM Labs, 44.5% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners said nobody formally validates their assessments.

Meanwhile, about 76% of organizations with more than 100 employees use some form of personality testing 17. Testing that widely with nothing checking the tests is how a normal hiring step turns into a legal problem.

The exposure gets wider under the ADA's "regarded as" prong. Since 2008, a candidate is covered if you act against them over an impairment you only think they have 20. Nobody has to prove a real limitation.

You will not think of yourself as screening for a health condition. That is the trap. A label built in health research does it for you.

Behavioral Interview Questions That Score Actions, Not Mood

Replace the impression with a score. Ask every candidate the same job-relevant questions, and rate each answer against written behavioral anchors. Sackett and colleagues' 2023 meta-analysis puts structured interview validity at.42 21. Nothing else on its own does better, not cognitive tests, work samples, or personality measures 21.

A 2025 meta-analysis by Wingate and colleagues covered 37 studies and 30,646 people. Structured interviews predicted task performance at ρ=.30, and contextual performance, meaning team collaboration and citizenship behavior, at ρ=.28 22. Those are two separate measures, not rival versions of the.42 above. The point holds: the method is validated against work outcomes, not against personality types.

Structure also shuts the door a label like Type D would walk through. Research in the Journal of Business and Psychology compared standardized formats with freeform conversations. The standard format cut bias effects by more than half, d =.23 against d =.59 23.

Behavioral questions do the same job at question level. They ask what a candidate did in one specific past situation, so attention moves off how the person came across.

Six steps build the interview:

  1. Pick the competencies the role really needs, about five, so every question is visibly about the job.
  2. Write 5-7 questions per competency set, all about past behavior: what happened, what you did, what came of it 24.
  3. Write the anchors before the interview, and never bend them afterwards to fit how someone performed 24.
  4. Calibrate the panel in a 30-minute session: walk each question, agree what a 2 sounds like next to a 4, and score a mock answer together 25.
  5. Score alone and write the rating down before anyone speaks in the debrief 26.
  6. Compare scores against the anchor, not against impressions, when two interviewers land on a 2 and a 4 for the same answer 27.

Every level in the rubric below describes something you can see in the answer. Warmth and delivery have nowhere to enter the score.

Probe a thin or hesitant answer. The one rule is that a follow-up never changes the rubric the answer is scored against 24. A guarded candidate who opens up under a good follow-up gives you the same evidence a talkative one volunteered 24.

Our topline survey with SHRM Labs found 45.8% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners call their process inconsistent, and doubt it picks the best candidate rather than the best interviewer. Writing your score down before the room starts talking is what stops that drift.

Type D Traits on the Job: Fit, Strengths, Risks

Once someone is on your team, you manage what you can see, not a diagnosis you are not qualified to make. None of the profile below was ever checked against job performance. Read it as behavior you might notice, not as anything it predicts.

Expect a recognizable cluster: sadness, a habit of spotting what is wrong first, and hard self-talk. Add irritation when work strays from the plan they laid out. Add staying away from social settings full of people they do not know 28.

The same profile has a second half managers notice less. These people tend to be organized, caring and thoughtful 28. A messy work environment bothers them, and they are often the last to give up on a task after everyone else has 28.

Fit depends on the work itself. Low-risk work with standard, non-negotiable steps is a strong match, and the routine steadies them rather than wearing them down 28. High turnover, or a place that keeps changing, is the opposite, because constant change unsettles them 28.

On a team, they bring systems and habits that pull messier colleagues into line, and they often hold a group steady under pressure 28. Watch two things. They need to feel useful and appreciated, and because they hold so much in, that willingness to help gets taken advantage of 28. The second is quieter: in meetings where speaking up is the job, silence reads as nothing to contribute.

Managing Type D Behavior Without Diagnosing It

If a new hire is reserved, quick to expect the worst, or floored by small setbacks, ask what is driving it. Not what to call it. Then support them toward more positive thinking, including training if that is what builds confidence 28.

Steering them toward counseling is not your call. Once such a request is tied to mental health, it can count as a medical examination 16. This takes time and patience, and part of the work is reminding the person that others in the organization value them 28.

Four moves do most of the work:

  • Match the task to the person: the stable, procedural work described above, in a low-turnover team where help is needed and valued, brings out their best 28. Assign it on how they actually perform, not on a label you pinned on them.
  • Ask what sits underneath: treat withdrawal or gloom as a signal about workload, clarity, or confidence. Ask before you interpret.
  • Set short-term goals: they sharpen what the person values and wants, and teach them to look ahead with more hope and confidence 6.
  • Change the channel, not the person: if you need input they will not offer in a group, ask in writing or one to one, and use what comes back.

It is ordinary management, applied patiently to behavior you can actually see.

Frequently asked questions

What are type A, B, C, D personalities?

Type A is competitive and time-driven, Type B relaxed, Type C cooperative and emotion-suppressing, Type D distressed. Type A and B came out of cardiology in the 1950s. None of the four were validated to predict job performance.

What is the best job for a type D personality?

There is no Type D job list, because the research never studied occupations. In practice, steady work that follows standard procedures suits this profile, and a low-turnover team suits it best 28.

What do I do if a candidate brings up a mental health condition in an interview?

Do not ask about it, and keep it out of your scoring. Stay on job tasks. If they ask for an accommodation, pass it to HR and finish the same scored interview.

Can I ask candidates to take a personality test before I make an offer?

Yes, if it measures work-relevant traits such as habits and preferences, and you can show they link to the job's tasks 18. A test built to spot a mental disorder counts as a medical examination 16. Those are barred before an offer.
