# Type D Personality: What the Research Supports for Hiring

A candidate came across as guarded and downbeat, and someone on the panel called it Type D. Now you have to decide whether that read counts for anything.

Type D exists, but it came out of cardiology, not hiring. Its famous claim, that it predicts death in heart patients, fell apart when researchers pooled 19 studies covering more than 11,000 patients. And the moment a read like that shapes a hiring decision, you pick up exposure under disability law. What holds up is a structured interview scored against criteria written in advance, plus a plan for managing the person once they start.

Quick answer

Type D means two traits at once: a steady pull toward negative emotions, and holding those feelings back for fear of disapproval. It is scored by a 14-item questionnaire, not judged by eye, and it was built to study heart patients. Its best-known claim, about survival after heart disease, weakened once bigger studies tested it.

Nothing in the evidence touches how well anyone does a job. Ask every candidate the same questions, score the answers against criteria you wrote in advance, then manage what the person actually does at work.

Type D Personality Traits: Both, or It Isn't Type D

Type D is one specific thing: negative affectivity and social inhibition in the same person, measured by a 14-item questionnaire called the DS14 12. Either trait on its own is not Type D. The D stands for distressed 3.

Negative affectivity is a steady tendency to feel worry, irritability, gloom, anger and anxiety, across time and situations 1. Social inhibition is holding those feelings and opinions back around other people, out of fear of being rejected or disapproved of 1. One is what a person feels. The other is what they do with it in front of an audience.

The label rests on both together, never on either alone 1. Someone who worries constantly but says so freely is not Type D. Neither is someone reserved and calm.

Johan Denollet, a professor of medical psychology at Tilburg University in the Netherlands, defined Type D in 1995 4. He built it from clinical observations of cardiac patients and existing personality theory 1. That origin decides everything a hiring manager might want to do with the label.

The DS14 has two 7-item scales, one per trait, each item scored 0 to 4 5. You are Type D only if you score 10 or more on both 6. Denollet set that cut-off as a median split in a sample of cardiac patients 6.

The Mortality Finding That Failed to Replicate

The finding that made Type D famous was that it predicts death in heart patients. Then researchers pooled 19 prospective studies and more than 11,000 cardiovascular disease patients, and found Type D is not a risk factor for mortality in that population at all 7.

The early numbers looked strong. Denollet followed cardiac rehabilitation and coronary artery surgery patients 8. In one group of over 300, about 27% of the Type D patients had died within 10 years, against 6% of the others 8.

Bigger studies could not reproduce that. A large heart-failure sample showed no prognostic value for mortality, and patients treated with intracoronary stents showed no link to major cardiac events. Reviewing the reversal, researchers concluded the earlier, smaller studies "may have inadvertently reached exaggerated or false conclusions" 9. A 2023 analysis found smaller studies reported bigger Type D effects than large ones, which the authors flagged as a possible sign of publication bias 10.

So even in cardiology, its home ground, the construct's headline claim is contested. A label whose best-supported finding fell apart under scrutiny is a poor thing to stretch into a use nobody has tested.

No Type D Study Ever Measured Job Performance

No study behind the Type D label has ever used job performance, hiring, or workplace conduct as an outcome. It was developed to predict cardiac events, mortality, and transplant rejection in heart patients 3. It tells you who gets sicker, not who does the work.

Some of the research does touch work, which is where the confusion starts. A 2010 review reports Type D linked to more absence-leave and higher vital exhaustion 11. Read that outcome again: self-reported tiredness and days missed, studied by health psychologists as signs of strain. Not a measure of how well anyone does a job.

The rest of the work-adjacent evidence sits further into clinical ground. A systematic review of eight studies found Type D strongly associated with suicidal thoughts and behaviors across populations and countries 12. That is a mental-health finding, and mental health is exactly what a hiring decision is neither equipped nor permitted to screen on.

The DS14 has never been tested against a job-performance or personnel-decision criterion at all. But the label looks like a personality type: a name, a two-letter code, a defined trait profile. That is how it keeps getting pulled into hiring conversations.

A Self-Report Questionnaire Is the Easiest Thing in Hiring to Game

The DS14 is a self-report questionnaire, and it asks about negative affectivity and social inhibition 3. Those are the two things a candidate is under the most pressure to hide from an employer. People answer with what they think an employer wants to hear, not their real disposition, so the score is shaky before you do anything with it 13.

None of this makes personality assessment worthless. Some instruments were built and validated for selection and hold up. Even for those, the standard guidance is that results add insight rather than make the hiring decision for you 14. Type D was never validated for employment decisions at all, so using it to screen skips that caution entirely.

Calling a Candidate Type D Can Cross the ADA Line

The moment a Type D read shapes a hiring decision, the problem stops being scientific and turns legal. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) generally bars employers from asking about health conditions before a conditional offer, and it defines a banned medical examination partly by whether a test is designed to reveal an impairment or mental health condition 15. Congress named invisible disabilities such as epilepsy, heart disease, and mental illness as exactly what it feared would get screened out this way 15.

The DS14 is an instrument whose entire evidence base is cardiovascular disease risk 5. The line the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) draws runs straight through that. Psychological tests designed to identify a mental disorder or impairment are medical examinations; tests measuring traits such as honesty, preferences and habits are not 16. Type D is a health-psychology construct, which puts it on the wrong side of the split.

You do not even have to hand out the DS14. In Kroll v. White Lake Ambulance Authority, the Sixth Circuit held that requiring an employee to attend counseling could count as a medical examination 16. What made it one was a record tying the request to mental-health issues, even though nobody used clinical language 16. Call a candidate guarded or distressed, then act on it, and it can read the same way.

A defensible screen has to clear a bar Type D cannot reach. Compliance guidance for personality testing asks you to show a clear link between the traits a test measures and the tasks of the job 17. Type D's validated links run to coronary disease and psychological distress. You are also expected to check whether your hiring tools screen out qualified people with disabilities 18.

You Never Have to Call It a Disability for the ADA to Apply

Since 2008, a candidate is covered if you act against them because of an actual or perceived impairment 19. They do not have to prove the impairment limits a major life activity, only that it is not both transitory and minor 19. So the question is never whether the candidate really has a condition. It is whether you acted because you thought they did 20.

Denollet has been explicit that Type D is not a psychiatric disorder and describes normal personality traits 1. That protects the construct. It does not protect you, because the test turns on your perception, not on the clinical literature.

This happens at scale. Roughly 76% of companies with more than 100 employees use some form of personality testing 21. In 2018 the EEOC settled with Best Buy over allegations that its application-stage assessments adversely affected candidates by race and national origin 22.

Personality itself is not protected, and courts accept legitimate fit concerns 23. That cover disappears the moment the trait works as a proxy for an impairment you think you see.

You Can't Spot Type D Personality in a Job Interview

Nothing you see in an interview tells you whether a candidate is Type D, because the two traits that define the label look exactly like ordinary nerves. Picture a stranger sitting across from three people who decide whether they get paid next month.

Guardedness in a high-stakes, evaluative meeting is not a psychological profile. It is the expected reaction to the room. Behavioral inhibition, one trait sometimes folded into Type D descriptions, is the tendency to withdraw from unfamiliar situations 8. A first-round interview is unfamiliar by definition.

What separates a trait from a bad hour is persistence, not intensity. Type D descriptions say that these people feel the traits more consistently over time than the general population, who feel a variety of them at times 8. Both halves of the profile stay relatively stable across a four-year period. One conversation cannot establish anything about four years.

And the label is not assigned by impression anyway. It comes from the DS14, a scored questionnaire compared against population norms built from 2,508 respondents 5. You are not holding that questionnaire, you have no comparison group, and you have met the person once.

Experienced interviewers will tell you manner is signal. Other experienced interviewers will tell them they are scoring nerves. The way out is to stop reading the room and start measuring the work.

Run a Structured Interview and Score the Answer

A structured interview means every candidate for the same role answers the same questions, in the same order, judged against the same scoring criteria written in advance 24. That format strips out the impressionistic read: the confident tone, the warmth, the easy rapport that would otherwise mark a quiet candidate down for reasons unrelated to the work.

The type of question matters as much as the order. Behavioral questions ask what someone actually did in a specific past situation, not what they would hypothetically do. Research puts them at 55% predictive of future on-the-job behavior, against 10% for traditional opinion-based interviewing 25. A question that demands a checkable account of past action moves your attention from how this person came across to what this person did.

With a guarded candidate, the bias almost never lives in the questions. It lives in how you score the answer.

Score the Answer, Not the Room

A rubric replaces "I liked her energy" with documented, comparable indicators at each level. Write the anchors before the interview, and never adjust them afterwards to fit how someone performed 26. Five to seven questions across roughly five core competencies is one recommended range 26.

ScoreRatingWhat the candidate's answer shows
5ExceptionalDetailed, specific example with measurable outcomes; mastery-level skill; articulates lessons learned
4StrongClear, relevant example with positive outcomes; solid competency, minor gaps in depth
3AdequateRelevant example but thin on detail or outcomes; baseline competency
2Below expectationsVague or off-topic example; does not explain specific actions or impact
1InsufficientNo relevant example, or the example reveals a significant skill gap

Read down that last column. Every anchor points at something you can check in the work: a specific example, a measurable outcome, reasoning the candidate can explain. None of them points at delivery.

A hesitant answer that names the actions taken and what changed scores a 4. A fluent, warm answer with no example in it scores a 2.

Push on a thin answer with follow-ups. The one rule is that a follow-up never changes the rubric the answer is scored against 26. A guarded first response that opens up under a good follow-up gives you the same evidence a chattier candidate volunteered unprompted, and it counts the same 26. Score the substance surfaced across the whole exchange, not how easily it came out 26.

You Hired Them. Now Manage the Behavior, Not the Label

Score the interview well and you make an offer. The label stops mattering the moment the person starts, because now you can watch what they actually do. Most managers stop at the assessment: the score names something, then nothing changes on Monday.

Here is the opposite, worked through one hire. Call her Priya, an operations analyst who is thorough, quiet in group settings, and quick to name what might go wrong. You have not typed her and you never will. Every move below answers something she has done.

  • Ask what is behind the flag. When Priya says a plan will not hold, ask which step she expects to break. Understand what drives the negative read before you try to redirect it 27.
  • Match the work to what she has shown you. Fitting the task and the team beats trying to change the person 27. Fit it to strengths she has demonstrated on the job, never to a profile you have read into her manner. Routing someone into narrower work because you have decided they are distressed is the perception-driven decision that carries the legal exposure above.
  • Name her as the systems owner. If the team struggles to stay organized, someone like Priya brings the tools and habits that pull everyone else into a system 27.
  • Set goals in two-week blocks, not annual ones. Short-term goal-setting builds clarity about what matters most, and teaches a person to look ahead with more hope and confidence 8. Give Priya one visible win by the end of the month.
  • Say out loud that the support role counts. Reassuring someone of their worth to the people around them takes time and patience, not a single onboarding chat 27.

Priya steadies a room in group settings 27, and creates the harmony that goes missing on teams focused only on hitting targets 27.

Watch one thing. A willingness to help can be taken advantage of, and it needs monitoring 27. The person who never says no is the person you have to protect. Check her load yourself instead of waiting for her to raise it, and none of that needs her DS14 score.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best job for a type D personality?

There is no research-backed match, because no Type D study has measured job performance. Popular guidance points to stable, procedural work on a supportive team 27, but nothing behind the label ever tested that. Hire for what the role actually asks.

What is the difference between type D personality and introversion?

Introversion is about where someone draws energy, and a quiet person can be perfectly content. Type D needs distress: steady negative emotion plus holding it back for fear of disapproval 1. A calm, reserved candidate is not Type D 1.

What are type A, B, C, D personalities?

Four labels from separate research traditions, not one system. Type A is driven and impatient, Type B relaxed, Type C compliant and emotion-suppressing, Type D negative and socially inhibited. None of them was built to predict job performance.

Is type D personality a mental illness?

No. Johan Denollet, who defined it, is explicit that Type D is not a psychiatric disorder and describes normal personality traits 1. It was studied as a risk marker in heart patients, not as a diagnosis.

Can type D personality change over time?

Both halves of the profile stay relatively stable across a four-year period. Stable is not the same as fixed. The label was never meant as a permanent verdict on anyone.
