Curriculum Vitae Statistics, Dated to Their Survey Year

2,260 words · 7 sections · 4 sources

Most curriculum vitae statistics reach a board deck without the one detail that makes them checkable: the year someone collected the data. The six-second resume scan is a 2012 number, and the same publisher measured 7.4 seconds when it re-ran the study in 2018. The 75% rejection rate blamed on screening software is worse. It came from a 2012 sales pitch by a startup that closed the next year. Pinned to the year each one was measured, some of these numbers hold up and several collapse.

Quick answer

Date every resume statistic before you put it in a deck. The famous scan-time figure was revised upward by its own author. The 75% rejection rate was never a study, and the real constraint is application volume, not the software. Self-reported lying rates swing 20 points because each survey asks a different question of a different sample. And most roundups skip AI-written resumes entirely, now about half of all applications.

Recruiter Time Spent Reading a Resume: 6 Seconds, or 1 Minute 34?

The famous numbers measure a first-pass scan. A second, longer pass follows, and the two get quoted as if they were one event.

The six-second scan is a 2012 eye-tracking study by TheLadders, timing recruiters up to their first fit-or-no-fit call. The same company re-ran it in 2018 with 30 recruiters over 10 weeks and got 7.4 seconds 1. Almost nobody cites the newer answer.

Neither study says how the recruiters were picked, how many resumes each one read, or what the jobs were. One critic points out that 7.4 seconds means 300 resumes in half an hour, a pace nobody works at.

Timed with the method disclosed, the numbers run longer. Jan Tegze, a recruiting director, showed three resumes to 114 recruiters in 2023:

Resume lengthAverage time
Shortest17 seconds
Mid-length27 seconds
Longest46 seconds

The fastest review took 12 seconds, the slowest two minutes and 27.

A 2025 study of 4,289 reviews by 312 recruiters separates the two passes: 11.2 seconds for the first scan, a median of one minute 34 for the full review. Attention went to experience (38%), summary (24%), skills (18%), education (11%) and layout (9%). Nearly two-thirds of it lands on two blocks the candidate wrote and nobody has checked.

Fit buys time, not white space. Metric-based achievements, matching keywords and a single-column layout hold a recruiter longer. A recent graduate applying for a senior role can be rejected in a fraction of a second.

Resume Rejection Rates: the 75% ATS Figure Was a 2012 Sales Pitch

Nearly every large employer runs an applicant tracking system (ATS), the software that stores and sorts applications. 97.8% of Fortune 500 companies have one, counted across all 500 rather than sampled. Below that tier it thins out: about 70% of large organizations, against 20% of small and mid-sized firms.

What the software does now is rank. AI reads the work history, years of experience and skills, then grades the candidate before a person opens the file. Platforms with active AI ranking cover 79.3% of Fortune 500 hiring. Ranking is not rejecting.

The 75% claim traces to a 2012 sales pitch by Preptel, a resume-optimization startup that closed in 2013. No method, no sample, no primary document anyone has produced. Pages still print it in 2026, usually crediting a magazine rather than a study. The 70% figure parked beside it is no better: undated, and its own source admits it covers screening by people too.

Ask the people running the systems and you get a different mechanism. Enhancv interviewed 25 US recruiters in late 2025, and 23 said their software does not auto-reject on formatting or content. Where a content rule existed, someone had switched it on deliberately, such as reject below a 75% match.

What does the rejecting is supply. On Workday, applications grew four times faster than openings through midyear 2024 2. Candidates filed 356 million applications there across 2024, up 26%, while the jobs they chased rose 7% 2. Workday handles close to 30% of US openings, so this is not one odd customer base 2.

So nothing rejected the resume at position 150. The role was filled at position 20 and nobody opened the rest. If you think a parser is discarding good people, you go shopping for a better parser. If 100 applications arrive for a job your recruiter can screen 20 of, formatting changes nothing.

Resume Lying: 32% in 2021, 44% and 64.2% in 2025

There is no single rate, only surveys. The two most-quoted 2025 figures differ by 20 points because they asked different questions of different people.

StandOut CVLate 2023, published 20252,102 US adults 3Ever lied about details, skills, experience or references64.2%

Read the fourth column and the gap explains itself. The 44% covers interviews and cover letters as well; on the resume alone it is 24%. The 64.2% counts anyone who ever misstated any of four separate things, so one rounded salary puts a person in the yes column. It is tagged 2025 although the fieldwork ran in late 2023.

The same claim gets stretched first in both waves. Years of experience led in 2021 at 46%, ahead of education at 44%, and led again in 2025 at 38%. It is the one claim a resume states as a bare number and an interview rarely tests.

Undated figures are worse than wide ones. The 93% stat is not a lying rate at all. It measures how many people know someone who lied, reported January 2020. The 88% figure applies only to the one in five applicants who say they understand ATS software. If you cannot name the publisher, the sample and the fieldwork window, leave the number out.

AI Wrote Half of 2025 Resumes, and 83% of Those Users Admit Exaggerating

In 2022, resumes drafted with ChatGPT were a rounding error. By late 2024, some technology companies put AI-assisted resumes at 30-40% of applications. Employ's 2025 report, based on more than 1,500 job seekers, found 52% use AI to write or review their resume.

That is down from 58% in 2024. The curve rose fast, then flattened.

Employers moved on the same timeline. In October 2024, 51% of 948 business leaders used AI somewhere in hiring, and 82% of those used it on resumes. The companion 68% figure was a stated intention in that survey, not a measured outcome, and it still gets republished as fact.

Among job seekers who use AI, 83% say they have used it to exaggerate or lie about their skills. The base matters: it counts the 58% who use AI, not everyone who applies, and it spans the resume, cover letter, application and skills assessment. Polishing wording is not the problem. Masking a skills gap is. 29% have used AI to complete a test assignment, and 28% to generate interview answers.

Three tells recur often enough to train a screening team on:

  • Uniform structure: every bullet opens with an action verb, because that is what the models produce.
  • Round numbers: "Reduced costs by 30%" turns up because these models favor round percentages.
  • Impossible claims: a large language model (software that predicts text without knowing whether it is true) cannot tell that "led a team of 40" is odd for an intern.

None of these proves a lie. Pick the quantified claim, ask what the number was before, and ask what broke on the way there.

Why AI Detection Is Not a Control You Can Plan Around

Three 2025 surveys get blended into a trend that does not exist. 74% of 1,000 hiring managers had run into an AI-generated application. A separate figure says 74% can spot AI content [c11 → c37]. 49% of 3,000 managers auto-dismiss resumes they suspect, and 62% of 925 HR workers reject AI resumes that lack personalization. Different questions, different samples, and none of them checks whether the manager was right.

The tools are worse. False positive rates of 30-50% mean they flag human-written resumes as machine-written. That is not a rejection reason you can defend to a candidate or a regulator. A candidate who generates a resume and then edits the wording usually passes. The old human signal is gone too: awkward phrasing used to mark a resume for a closer read, and now everything arrives polished.

Background Checks Catch Discrepancies in 3 of 4 Screenings

94% of employers run background checks and 70% screen social media. Degrees are looser: 53% always verify one and 24% sometimes. So nearly a quarter never check the credential people are most tempted to embellish.

They find things. More than three-quarters of organizations hit at least one candidate discrepancy in the previous 12 months, per HireRight's 2025 report of over 1,000 HR, risk and talent professionals 4. Employment history and education were flagged most often in every region. Only 7% of employers with 5,000 or more staff had a clean year, against 41% of small and mid-sized firms 4. Read that as a volume effect, not a character difference.

A discrepancy is not a lie. People misremember their own start and end months, and verification records carry their own errors.

Getting caught still costs. 41% of offers are withdrawn and 18% of people already hired are fired. A ResumeLab survey found 65% of caught candidates were not hired or were terminated. Both figures land after the offer, when restarting the search is expensive.

Frequently asked questions

Can ATS detect ChatGPT resume?

Not reliably. Most applicant tracking systems parse and rank, they do not run AI detection. Bolt-on detectors flag 30-50% of human-written resumes as machine-written, and a lightly edited AI resume usually passes.

What are red flags on a CV?

Unexplained gaps, dates given only in years, titles that do not match the work described, and round-number achievements with no before figure. None proves dishonesty. Treat each as a question to ask, not a reason to reject.

How can you verify a resume claim before making an offer?

Ask the candidate to produce the work behind one quantified claim. A short structured skills test or a live problem checks the ability itself, at screening. Background checks confirm history, but usually run after the offer.

Is a CV the same as a resume?

In the US, people use both words for a one or two page hiring document. In the UK, Europe and academia, a CV is the standard term and runs longer, listing publications, teaching and full career history.
