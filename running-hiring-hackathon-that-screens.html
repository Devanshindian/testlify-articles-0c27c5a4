Hackathon Judging: The Rubric and AI-Use Policy

3,926 words · 10 sections · 9 sources

A hiring hackathon ends with a room full of demos and no agreed way to rank them. Two judges watch the same build and disagree, and hackathon judging has no written rule to settle it. In our research with SHRM Labs, 45.8% of HR and TA practitioners said hiring confidence varies by hiring manager or role.

Nobody wrote down the AI rules either, so two submissions can arrive with no way to tell what produced them. Fix the dimensions, the weights and the AI policy before anyone starts building, and the score becomes evidence you can defend.

What counts as a hiring hackathon (and what doesn't)

A hiring hackathon is a timed, scenario-based work sample inside your funnel. Candidates build something realistic under a fixed brief, in a fixed window, and the output feeds an advancement decision. That hiring consequence separates it from a public competition, student event, internal innovation day, or security challenge. Those events may rank, teach, brand, or entertain. Yours has to support a hiring decision.

Ask candidates to build features in a realistic environment and you are close to a work sample test in the industrial and organizational psychology sense. That is useful, but it raises the bar. A demo-day contest can be judged on taste. A screening step needs job-linked criteria, a recorded score, and evidence a hiring team can explain later.

The advantage is that the task looks like the job. Work samples tend to carry lower adverse impact risk than many alternative screens 1, and they fit content-validity requirements when they come from job tasks 1. Candidates also tend to accept them because the link to the role is visible 1. Those benefits only hold when scoring uses predetermined, job-linked criteria rather than guesswork 1. That is why the event design has to turn quickly into a scoring design.

Decide what the hackathon score is allowed to decide

Before anyone builds, write down what the score is allowed to decide. A hackathon feels objective because candidates ship artifacts, but the hiring signal comes from fixed criteria, not from the demo. For a hiring event, the answer should be narrow: does this person advance in the funnel, and on what evidence.

Without a defined hackathon judging plan, judges apply criteria inconsistently. A project's rating can reflect which judge happened to watch it, not what the candidate built. Fix the weights and evidence rules in advance, and you buy back fairness, efficiency, consistency, and credibility with candidates.

That inconsistency is already common, as the SHRM Labs figure above shows. Structured, criteria-based evaluation beats gut-feel judgment, and breaking vague quality into measurable parts is what makes scores comparable across judges.

Pick 3-6 job-linked dimensions and fix weights first

Build the rubric from the job description, not from what looks impressive on demo day. For a work-sample build, name the skills the exercise is meant to test. Rubric guidance lands in a narrow range, three to six dimensions. Go higher and judges tire, which lowers consistency; go lower and the rubric is too blunt to show what went wrong.

Here is the order to work in:

  1. Pull the skills from the job description. Name the things the challenge tests. Check that each one is visible in a submission.
  2. Name the dimensions. Draw from accuracy, instruction adherence, completeness, safety, reasoning quality, and tone or style. Rename them in the language of the role.
  3. Pick one scale and hold it. Everything that follows here uses one to five, with a written definition at every point.
  4. Set the weights before the event, not after the demos.
  5. Write the evidence rules. State whether judges may use only the submission, whether missing documentation costs points, and whether they may infer intent.
  6. Decide the edge cases in writing. A correct build that ignores a stated constraint has to fail somewhere. Say whether that lands on instruction adherence, completeness, or both.

Tight evidence rules cut disagreement between judges. Keep at least one dimension aimed at reasoning, not only the artifact. Otherwise polish can outscore thinking. Then put the task, dimensions, allowed evidence, and adjudication notes into one short template every judge reads. Keep it short enough to survive a long judging day.

A weighted hackathon judging rubric you can copy

Here is the hackathon judging rubric. Copy it, swap the dimensions for the ones your role needs, and keep the weights fixed before anyone starts building. Judging across hackathon formats tends to use a small set of criteria: technical execution, innovation or originality, impact and usefulness, design, completeness, and presentation. What changes is the weighting, which should match what your hiring process rewards.

For an AI-build challenge, the useful split is evidence, feasibility, and workflow fit. Novelty should count only when it supports something shippable. Score each dimension 1-5, multiply by the weight, then sum the weighted scores for the candidate's total. Use a spreadsheet or scoring tool, and sanity-check totals before ranking anyone.

Most panels skip the anchors below. A bare instruction to score from 1 to 5 is not a usable rubric. Written anchors, with a worked example attached to each band, keep a 5 meaning the same thing to every judge.

Give judges one pre-graded example at each end of the scale before they score. Seeing a top and a bottom submission improves consistency more than wording alone. A working-but-plain build with a clear rationale should beat a polished one nobody can explain.

How to calibrate judges so scores actually agree

Judges score privately first, and compare only after every candidate has been rated. That sequencing rule stops one opinion from anchoring the room before anyone has looked at the evidence. Assign at least two independent reviewers to each submission, and keep their scores hidden until the last candidate is scored 2. Give each judge a private scoring view rather than a shared editing surface. Independence is the cheapest fix for judging that swings with whoever is in the room.

Run a short calibration pass before the event

Calibration happens after the rubric is written and before live judging opens. The rubric says what good looks like; calibration proves the panel applies that definition the same way. Skip it and the disagreement surfaces later, when it costs more to fix.

  1. Pick sample submissions that span the quality range. A facilitator selects examples from weak to strong, so the panel applies the whole scale.
  2. Have each judge score one or two anonymized samples alone. Past candidate work, redacted notes, or seeded practice builds all work. Score them against the rubric with no discussion.
  3. Walk the scale and the criteria tiers together first. Cover must-have, nice-to-have, and bonus criteria. Use concrete examples until everyone uses the same words for the same behaviors.
  4. Put every judge's score for the same sample on one sheet. A 2 and a 5 from two judges on the same criterion is the gap you examine.
  5. Make each judge justify their rating with cited evidence. The feeling of a score is not a justification; only a specific behavior or artifact counts 3.
  6. Rewrite the ambiguous rubric language, then stop. The goal is sharper wording before live judging starts, not one correct score for the practice item.

You will usually find one or two criteria that need tighter definitions. One judge may treat a clickable prototype as proof the thing works. Another may want evidence from the person who owns the workflow. The split stays invisible until the scores sit side by side.

Check agreement instead of assuming it

A rubric that reads well on paper can still produce scattered scores. Every submission already carries two independent scores, so pull a slice of those pairs, commonly 10-20%, and adjudicate the disagreements instead of letting them pass. Measure agreement formally: Cohen's kappa for two reviewers, Krippendorff's alpha when you have more raters or missing ratings. An alpha at or above 0.800 is the usual reliability convention. A range from 0.667 to 0.800 may be acceptable for tentative calls, depending on what the decision costs.

That is the stricter bar. In recruiting practice the floor is lower: inter-rater reliability below 0.40 is treated as a warning and tracked monthly. When agreement is low, pushing reviewers to try harder does not work. Raters splitting by more than one point on the scale are telling you the rubric needs revision 3.

Adjudicate split scores, then revise the rubric

Resolve a disagreement through discussion or a third reviewer, never by averaging the two numbers or taking a majority 2. Then track which criteria split reviewers most often. A criterion that repeatedly divides a panel is either ambiguous or subjective 2. The usual defects are overlapping dimensions, where quality, impact, and completeness partly mean the same thing, and weak anchors that offer adjectives instead of thresholds.

Write down each adjudication and its reasoning. That log becomes the precedent for the next ambiguous submission and the source material for the next rubric revision. Save the calibrated rubric to a shared system, so the reference is the document rather than each judge's memory.

Calibration does not end at the first event. Average scores drift after repeated rounds on the same role, either inflating toward strong yes or compressing toward the middle. A quarterly re-anchoring session fixes that, and any judge who joins later runs a short version before scoring a live submission.

Review the borderline scores, and keep every judge's notes

Treat the rubric total as one input to a human decision, never the decision itself. One hackathon evaluation process has staff review every flagged submission and borderline case before advancement is final. Its stated policy is that "automated scores are inputs to human judgment, not the sole determining factor" 4.

Redundancy is what makes that review more than a rubber stamp. Use a second-reader policy on borderline scores, where a judge who has not scored that submission reads the evidence independently before a close call is finalized. Combine the build with other evaluation formats rather than resting a decision on one judge's read of one artifact. When two candidates finish close, that wider evidence shows the tradeoffs between them instead of leaving demo feel to decide.

Ask judges for a short rationale quoting the specific evidence, especially on low scores. A number with no stated reason cannot be used later to fix an anchor or a reviewer's calibration. Reasoning first, score second produces more accurate evaluations and leaves an audit trail.

A defensible record needs more than the point total. Judging tools built for sponsor disputes give you a downloadable, per-judge breakdown rather than an aggregate leaderboard. That breakdown shows which judge scored which criterion how, instead of asking the panel to remember.

Give the records a retention period tied to a reason. One hackathon holds qualifier scores and feedback through a fixed appeals date. It keeps advancement decisions for the same window, then keeps anonymous score distributions up to two years for program improvement 4. Keep rubric scores and AI-disclosure records long enough to answer an appeal or a later question about why someone did not advance. AI-hiring regulation increasingly expects explainable, per-candidate score breakdowns, and that trail supplies them.

A blanket AI ban breaks; saying nothing breaks worse

Write the AI rules before anyone starts building, because both easy defaults fail. One reported policy asks candidates not to use AI at any point in the application or interview process. It allows disqualification during candidacy and termination if the use surfaces after hire 5. Commentators call that extreme, since using AI to sharpen applications and practice for interviews is now an ordinary job-market skill 5. Candidates say so too: a blanket ban reads as distrust, and some pass on the employer entirely 5.

Silence costs more. A 2023 survey found almost half of job seekers were already using ChatGPT to write resumes and cover letters, and 70% reported a higher response rate 6. Legal guidance notes this use often goes undetected by employers 6. Our own research with SHRM Labs found 73.4% of HR and TA practitioners are concerned about candidates using AI during interviews or assessments. Without a stated policy, you cannot compare two submissions fairly, because you do not know what produced either one.

Disclosure alone does not fix that. Across 16 studies with more than 27,000 participants, text labeled as AI-assisted was rated lower than identical text labeled human-written. A pre-registered survey found the same split: people call disclosure important, then penalize the disclosed work anyway 7. A policy promising no disadvantage for disclosed use will not hold unless judges know exactly how to score it. The workable position is to allow the tool, then make the candidate defend what it produced.

The AI-use policy you can hand candidates

The line your policy has to draw is preparation versus real-time substitution of the candidate's own judgment. Using AI to polish a resume or rehearse for an interview is treated as unremarkable 5. What draws a hard line is an answer to a live, role-specific scenario that is "100% AI and not of their own thought" 5. Candidates cannot calibrate to that line unless you write it down before the challenge starts.

Publish the tier before anyone builds, because the rules that score a submission had to be visible while the candidate was building. Employment counsel's general guidance applies directly: any restriction on AI use needs a nondiscriminatory business reason attached in advance 6. If you run more than one track, post rules per track. A senior engineering track and a product track have different reasons to restrict 6.

Define what AI may and may not generate

The workable split is between AI as scaffolding over the candidate's own work and AI generating the deliverable's substance wholesale. Boilerplate is the carve-out. If deleting a block of code leaves something replaceable by an internet template in five minutes, it is boilerplate. If deleting it breaks something only your product does, that is the feature work where your judgment signal lives.

Require a disclosure statement judges can actually score

A scorable disclosure names the tool, states the task it performed, and confirms a human reviewed the output before submission. Keep it to a mandatory field in the submission form, not a line buried in terms language the candidate skims. Force each field rather than accept free text, because missing elements are the most common reason disclosure statements get returned in academic settings.

Ask for it category by category: scaffolding, debugging, code review, and documentation, with the tool named for each 9. Each category maps to a different rubric line, so judges can see where AI entered the work. Depth should scale with influence. Editing-only help gets a short confirming line; AI that shaped core logic requires naming which functions and files it touched.

Score the disclosure line item on those three elements, as a check that sits outside the weighted dimensions so the weights stay fixed. A candidate who writes "used AI to help" without naming the tool or task scores lower than one who itemizes where AI entered the build.

Set consequences for missing or false disclosure

State the consequence in writing before the event: failure to disclose, or deliberate misrepresentation, may result in disqualification from the process or withdrawal of an offer 8. Published hiring rules already pair a stage-specific prohibition with an explicit warning, and yours should read the same way. Undisclosed AI use found after the fact triggers a defined point deduction or disqualification rule written into the rubric in advance.

Say the other half just as plainly, because candidates will not disclose honestly if disclosure feels like a trap. One AI hackathon puts it in a single line: using AI tools with proper disclosure does not disadvantage your submission. Disclosed use in permitted areas is scored on execution quality, with no blanket penalty for the disclosure itself. Because labelled work gets rated lower than identical unlabelled work by default, that promise only holds if judges score disclosed submissions against the same anchors as every other submission, and if you check that they do.

How to verify a candidate actually built it

Verification happens in a live conversation rather than a detection tool. AI can produce polished-looking work across many scenarios, so a follow-up discussion decides whether the candidate understands what they submitted. Ask them to walk through one hard decision, explain a rejected trade-off, and change something small on the spot. That discussion catches undisclosed or misrepresented AI use better than a separate compliance check.

Written materials still give you prompts for that discussion. Flag repeated phrases, generic content that misses the specific task, formatting inconsistencies, shifting tone, language more complex than the task warrants, and similarities across candidates 6. Another signal is an answer structured to score maximally against a known rubric, such as complexity analysis volunteered on an algorithm question. Human work usually shows the mess of getting there, while AI-generated solutions arrive fully formed with no visible dead ends. Look for iteration evidence: commit history, notes on abandoned approaches, documented trade-offs. Treat those traces as prompts, not proof, because a tidy commit history is easy to fake.

Where AI use is part of the job, set the parameters explicitly rather than leaving candidates to guess the boundary. Score the behavior, not the tool: prompting and iteration shown, output verified and debugged by the candidate, disclosure complete and specific. A rubric resists gaming when every criterion has anchored score bands and a required evidence component. And watch your own judges. If one quietly down-scores any submission that admits AI use, the disclosure rule stops working. Every score should trace back to the specific evidence that produced it.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a hiring hackathon run?

Four to eight hours for a screening round, and no more than a weekend for a final stage. Longer events select for free time rather than skill, and they shrink your candidate pool.

What is the difference between a hiring hackathon and a take-home assignment?

A hackathon is timed, so you see how someone works under pressure. A take-home is private and untimed, so the window is wider. Neither one bounds AI use on its own, which is why both need the same rubric and the same disclosure rules.

Should you pay candidates for a hiring hackathon?

Pay for anything past a few hours, and for any output you could actually ship. Only a round short enough to stay under that bar goes unpaid, so a full-day build is not one. Prizes are not a substitute, because only the winners ever receive one.

Do hiring hackathons work for non-technical roles?

Yes, when the brief is a work product: a campaign plan, a support escalation, a pricing case. Score it with the same weighted rubric. The format is the timing, not the code.

How do you give feedback to candidates who do not advance after a hackathon?

Send the rubric dimensions and where the submission fell short, drawn from the judges' written rationales. Keep it behavior-based. Do not rank them against other candidates or hint at a future round.

A hackathon only screens when the rubric, the weights and the AI rules are fixed before the first commit. Everything after that is bookkeeping: private scores, a calibration pass, and a written reason behind every number.
