The Real Cost Per Hire in 2026: $4,700 Is Two Editions Old

2,766 words · 7 sections · 4 sources

Ask three executives what a hire costs and you get three numbers, each said with confidence. Every cost per hire figure comes with a report, a year and a method, and most of the ones in circulation have lost all three. The $4,700 still anchoring budget decks describes a hiring market that is gone.

The invoices tell you less than half the story anyway. Roughly 60% of a hire's cost is manager and interviewer time that no accounting system records. The number you can defend is the current one, sourced, split into both halves, and read by role and by hiring volume.

Quick answer

Cost per hire is what you spend to source, assess and hire one person, divided by the hires made in the same period. The benchmark most teams quote comes from a superseded SHRM cycle. The current one is $5,475 for a non-executive hire. Current releases also mix averages with medians, so figures for the same year can look like they contradict each other. Either way, the figure counts only what someone billed you for. The screening and interview hours behind a hire cost more than the invoices do. Build the number by role band and by hiring volume, and name the report and year behind every figure you quote.

The $4,700 Cost Per Hire Is Two SHRM Editions Old

The current benchmark for a non-executive hire is $5,475, from SHRM's 2025 Benchmarking Report of October 15, 2025. Plan your 2026 budget against that one. SHRM refreshes the series each fourth quarter, so the next edition lands around October 2026.

The $4,700 is not wrong. It is expired. It comes from a SHRM article of April 11, 2022, built on a survey that ran from April to November 2021. SHRM's 2025 release says plainly that it updates the 2022 work 1. Older still is the $4,129 you also see quoted, which covers fiscal year 2015. Two dead cycles, a decade apart, both passed off as live.

Even the current numbers look like they clash. SHRM publishes an average in one report and a median in another, and the two measure different things. Here is what each headline figure rests on.

The executive median went from $5,000 in 2017 to $10,600 in 2025 and $15,000 in 2026. Non-executive costs barely moved 2. SHRM puts that flatness down to a cooling job market, not a change in method. It is also why the stale figure survived so long. For most roles a team fills, it was close enough. At the executive tier it is now badly wrong.

How to Calculate Cost Per Hire, and Where It Stops

One definition sits under every figure above. Cost per hire is the total internal and external spend needed to source, attract, assess and hire one new employee, divided by the hires you made in the same period.

CPH = (Total Internal Recruiting Costs + Total External Recruiting Costs) / Total Number of Hires

Internal is what your own organization pays for:

  • Recruiter salaries and loaded overhead
  • Hiring manager and interviewer time
  • HR administrative time
  • Recruiting technology
  • Referral bonuses

External is what you pay someone else:

  • Job board postings and advertising
  • Agency or retained search fees
  • Background checks and assessments
  • Sign-on bonuses and relocation

The boundary matters more than either list. Under the SHRM standard, counting stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding sits outside it.

One quarter, worked: 15 hires, $30,000 internal, $25,000 external. That is $55,000 over 15 hires, or $3,667 each.

Hard Costs vs Soft Costs: 30-40% Is All You See

Only 30-40% of what a hire costs arrives as an invoice. The rest, roughly 60%, is the time department leaders and managers spend screening applications and running interviews. Build your figure from receipts and you capture well under half of it.

Hard costs are easy to name because someone billed you: job ads, agency and search fees, assessment tools, recruiting software, relocation, employer payroll taxes. Each lands in the ledger with a date and an amount.

Soft costs hide inside salaries and calendars you already pay for. A manager who spends six hours on interviews this week costs exactly what they cost last week. The money moved. Nothing recorded that it did.

That is why most calculators stop at the invoice. A finance system has no field for an engineer's afternoon. The difference is visibility, not importance. Work from receipts alone and you spend your effort on the smaller half of the bill.

Agency fees: 15-25% of salary, 40-50% at C-level

For professional and senior roles, the agency fee is the single largest external cost, and it scales with salary rather than sitting at a fixed price. Published bands run 15-25% of first-year base at manager through VP level, and other benchmarks stretch that to 30%. Quoted deals cluster at the top. A fee agreed before the search starts is rarely renegotiated once a candidate is chosen.

On a $120,000 role that is $18,000 to $36,000 for the fee alone. One line, bigger than most published benchmarks for an entire hire. It explains most of the distance between an entry-level hire and an executive one.

Soft Costs: 23 Interview Hours per Hire

The uninvoiced side is time, and it is the larger half. It covers interviewer hours, output lost while a seat sits empty, ramp time for the new person, and the fallout when a hire does not work out. Only the interview hours sit inside the count, because counting stops at the accepted offer.

Two of these dominate, and both are hours.

The first is interview time, the largest hidden cost in most hiring budgets. A technical hire averages 23.3 hours of interview time against 12.2 hours for a business hire 3. Data roles run highest at 24.9 hours, customer support lowest at 8.9 3.

Price those hours and they become money. A recruiter with 15 open roles and three interviews per role per round burns more than 45 hours of senior calendar time before an offer goes out. At a blended $100 an hour, that is $4,500 in interview labor. Every input is a rate you already pay, so the figure survives a budget review.

Technical roles now average 17.6 interviews per hire, up 52% from roughly 11 in 2021. Batching interviews into one scheduled event, instead of weeks of calendar back-and-forth, is the main lever you have.

The second is volume, and nobody added headcount to absorb it. Recruiters now handle about 291 applications per hire, up from roughly 100 in early 2021 3, with workloads climbing 93% year over year 4. Much of that inbound is duplicated or auto-generated, so the extra volume buys screening hours rather than candidates.

Every hour spent triaging a bigger pile is an hour not spent closing a finalist, which is the work that brings cost down. In our topline report with SHRM Labs, 25.2% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners said they have never measured how much recruiter time goes to screening versus strategic work.

Two things push a role up its band, and only one of them shows on an invoice. When skills are scarce, recruiters spend more hours sourcing and teams lean harder on paid channels and agencies. Senior and specialized roles also need more rounds and longer sourcing cycles, which raises recruiter and hiring-manager time per hire.

Hiring Volume, Not Company Size, Sets What You Pay per Hire

Seniority is one axis. Volume is the other. Much of your recruiting spend is fixed, and the hire count you divide it by is not. Same budget, very different cost per hire.

A 200-person company making 40 hires on $120,000 of recruiting spend lands at $3,000 each. A company with fewer than 50 employees often runs past $7,500. It has no dedicated recruiting team, so it leans on agencies and paid postings, and still pays more per role than a bigger employer. An enterprise spreads ATS licences, recruiter salaries and branding across hundreds of hires.

So a small hiring budget is not proof of efficient hiring. Usually it is proof of a small denominator.

The breakeven sits near 15 hires a year

Whether you source directly or pay agencies comes down to volume. An in-house function costs $146,000 to $200,000 a year to run whether it fills 30 roles or three. Agency spend is purely variable. Below roughly 15-20 hires a year the agency is cheaper, and above it the fixed team wins.

Treat that as a midpoint, not a threshold. Published estimates run from six or seven hires up to 30 or 40, mostly because they price different things: one recruiter with a lean tool stack, or a full talent acquisition function with sourcing and admin. Two questions settle your own crossover. Is demand steady enough to keep a recruiter busy through a slow quarter? And are the roles specialized enough that an internal generalist cannot reach the candidates at all?

The Vacancy Cost the Formula Leaves Out: $500 a Day

An empty seat costs you money every day it stays empty, and the SHRM/ANSI formula counts none of it. For an average professional role, that is about $500 a day in lost output. Set that against SHRM's 2026 median time to fill of 39 days for non-executive roles and the seat costs roughly $19,500 before a single invoice arrives 2. Sales roles can lose $7,000 to $10,000 a month in deals nobody closed. Executive searches run longer, at a median of 45 days.

Pressure to fill fast pushes you toward agencies and sponsored listings, the most expensive channels available. A slow process raises both halves of the number at once.

Four rules keep your figure defensible:

  • Match the periods. Annual spend over a quarterly hire count is not a cost per hire. Count hires whole, never as fractions of a full-time equivalent.
  • Segment before you compare. Average a bulk hiring push with one specialized search and you misstate both.
  • Include the searches that failed. All recruiting costs in a period, over all hires in that period.
  • Read it next to quality of hire. Only 20% of organizations track both, and a cheap hire who leaves at month six was never cheap 1.

The first three protect the arithmetic. The fourth protects the decision, because cost falls fastest when the bar drops.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average cost per hire in the US?

The current SHRM benchmark is $5,475 for a non-executive hire, an average from the 2025 Benchmarking Report. The U.S. median sits near $1,633, because high-volume entry-level roles pull it down.

How do you lower cost per hire without dropping hiring quality?

Cut interview hours before you cut spend. Screen harder earlier, batch your interview panels, and pay for referrals. Referred hires cost 40-60% less than agency placements and stay longer.

Does cost per hire include onboarding costs?

No. Under the SHRM standard the count stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding sits outside it. Track training and ramp time separately, or the two figures stop being comparable across periods.

What is the difference between cost per hire and time to fill?

Cost per hire measures money spent per filled seat. Time to fill measures the days from open requisition to accepted offer. A slow fill raises cost, because an empty professional seat loses roughly $500 a day.

How much does it cost to replace an employee who leaves?

Replacement is a separate metric, and folding it into cost per hire breaks the comparison. Common estimates run from six to nine months of the leaver's salary, and climb sharply for specialized and senior roles.
