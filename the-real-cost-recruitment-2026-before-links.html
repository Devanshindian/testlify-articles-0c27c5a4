# The Real Cost Per Hire in 2026: $4,700 Is Two Editions Old

Ask three executives what a hire costs and you get three different numbers, each quoted with confidence. Every cost per hire figure carries a report, a year and a method, and most of the ones in circulation have lost all three. The $4,700 still anchoring budget decks describes a hiring market that no longer exists.

The invoices tell you less than half the story anyway. Roughly 60% of what a hire costs is interviewer and manager time that no accounting system records.

Quick answer

Cost per hire is what you spend to source, assess and hire one person, divided by the hires you made in the same period. The current benchmark is $5,475 for a non-executive hire. The $4,700 most teams quote comes from a superseded SHRM cycle. Current releases also mix averages with medians, so two figures for the same year can look like they contradict each other. Either way, the figure only counts what someone billed you for, and the interview hours behind a hire cost more than the invoices do. Build your own number by role band and by hiring volume, and name the report and year behind every figure you quote.

The $4,700 Cost Per Hire Benchmark Is Two SHRM Editions Old

The current benchmark for a non-executive hire is $5,475, from SHRM's 2025 Benchmarking Report released October 15, 2025 1. Plan your 2026 budget against that one. SHRM refreshes the series in the fourth quarter each year, so the next edition is not due until around October 2026 1.

The $4,700 is not wrong so much as expired. It comes from a SHRM article published April 11, 2022 2. The survey behind it ran from April through November 2021, so the number describes hiring conditions now roughly five years old 3. SHRM's 2025 release says plainly that the new study updates its prior 2022 work 4. Older still is the $4,129 you also see quoted, covering fiscal year 2015 and published August 8, 2016 5. Two superseded cycles, a decade apart, both passed around as if they were live.

Why SHRM reports $35,879 and $10,600 for the same hire

Both are real, current SHRM numbers. One is an average, the other a median, and they measure different things about the same tier of hire 6. Set one against the other and you get a contradiction that does not exist.

The samples moved too. The 2025 averages come from 2,371 respondents surveyed January 9 to March 3, 2025 4; the 2026 medians from over 4,600 organizations, with data through November 24, 2025 9.

The executive median climbed from $5,000 in 2017 to $10,600 in 2025, then $15,000 in 2026 6. Non-executive costs barely moved across that same series 9, which SHRM puts down to a cooling job market rather than any change in method. That flatness is why a stale figure survived so long. For most roles a team fills, it had not moved. At the executive tier it is now wrong by a wide margin.

How to Calculate Cost Per Hire: The Formula, and What Counts as a Cost

Cost per hire is the total internal and external spend to source, attract, assess and hire one new employee, divided by the number of hires in the same period 10. SHRM and ANSI treat one formula as the standard:

Cost per hire = (total internal recruiting costs + total external recruiting costs) ÷ total number of hires 10

SideWhat goes in it
Internal, what you pay your own people and systemsRecruiter salaries and loaded overhead, hiring manager and interviewer time, HR admin time, recruiting technology, referral bonuses 10
External, what you pay someone elseJob board postings, advertising, agency or retained search fees, background checks and assessments, sign-on bonuses, relocation 10

The boundary matters more than either list. Under the SHRM standard the count stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding sits outside it 11.

One quarter, worked: 15 hires, $30,000 internal, $25,000 external. That is $55,000 over 15 hires, or $3,667 each 10.

Hard Costs vs Soft Costs: The Invoice Is Only 30-40% of the Bill

Only 30-40% of what a hire costs arrives as an invoice. The rest, roughly 60%, is the hours your department leaders and managers spend screening applications and running interviews 2. So a figure built from receipts captures well under half the real spend 12.

Hard costs are easy to name because someone billed you for them: job ads, agency and search-firm fees, screening and assessment tools, recruiting software, relocation packages, employer payroll taxes. Soft costs sit inside salaries and calendars you already pay for, so no separate charge ever appears 13. A manager who spends six hours on interviews this week costs exactly what they cost last week. The money moved and nothing recorded that it did.

That is why most calculators stop at the invoice. They run on the fields a finance system can export, and finance has no field for an engineer's afternoon. The difference is visibility, not importance 14.

Three things to watch on those lines. Your applicant tracking system (ATS) fee spreads across the hires you make in the same period, never a longer one. Verifications are charged per employer and criminal searches per county, so a candidate who has moved four times in seven years costs several times more to screen 18. And relocation reimbursements are taxable wages, so a gross-up adds about 42% on top 19.

Job board budgets are spent per click, not per hire, so the same money buys a different number of hires each quarter. That makes ad spend the least reliable input in an otherwise predictable half of the budget.

Agency Fees Run 15-25% of Salary, 40-50% at C-Level

Third-party agency fees are the largest external cost for professional and senior roles, and they scale with salary rather than sitting at a fixed price 7. The published band is 15-25% of first-year base salary from manager through VP 20, and other benchmarks stretch it to 30% 21. Quoted deals cluster at the top of that range. Settle the fee before the search starts, because no recruiter will reopen it once you have picked someone.

On a $120,000 role, the fee alone runs $18,000 to $36,000 22. That single line is bigger than most published cost per hire benchmarks for an entire hire, and it explains most of the distance between an entry-level number and an executive one. Junior roles often skip the agency altogether. Senior and specialized roles rarely can.

  • Contingency, the mid-level default: you pay only if a placement happens, and you can run several firms at once. The trade is less dedicated attention and weaker reach into candidates who are not looking 23.
  • Container: part paid upfront to secure recruiter time, the balance due on placement 24.
  • Retained: exclusive, paid in installments tied to completed stages, around 30-35% of salary 25.
  • C-level: the highest fees in the market, reaching 40-50% of first-year income 26, plus administrative fees and search expenses on top 26. Budget a range, not a rate.

Referral bonuses commonly land between $500 and $5,000 per hire 27. Set that against a 20% fee on a $75,000 placement, which is $15,000 to a single vendor 28. Referred hires also cost 40-60% less than agency placements and stay longer 27. When a referral program fails, the reason is procedural rather than financial: pay the bonus within 30 days of the start date, and keep the nomination to one form 29.

Soft Costs: 23 Interview Hours per Technical Hire

Indirect costs are the expenses that never reach an invoice but still land on the bottom line: interviewer and hiring-manager hours, output lost while a seat sits empty, ramp time for the new person, and the fallout when a hire does not work out 14. Only the first sits inside the cost per hire count. Ramp time falls outside it, and replacement is a separate metric again.

Interview time is the largest hidden line, and it scales with the role. A technical hire averages 23.3 hours of total interview time against 12.2 hours for a business hire 30. By function, data roles run highest at 24.9 hours and customer support lowest at 8.9 30. Technical roles now average 17.6 interviews per hire, up 52% from roughly 11 in 2021 31.

Price those hours and they become a budget line. A recruiter running 15 open roles with three interviews per role per round burns more than 45 hours of senior calendar time before a single offer goes out. At a blended loaded rate of $100 an hour, that is $4,500 in interview labor across those 15 searches 29. Every input is a rate you already pay, which is why the figure survives a budget review. Batching interviews into one scheduled event, instead of spreading them over weeks of calendar back-and-forth, is the main lever you have here 11.

Volume is the second cost, and nobody added headcount to absorb it. Recruiters now process roughly 291 applications per hire, up from about 100 in early 2021 30, with workloads climbing 93% year over year 32. Much of that inbound is duplicated or auto-generated, so the extra volume buys screening hours rather than candidates. Every hour spent triaging a bigger pile is an hour not spent closing a finalist, which is the work that brings the number down. In our topline report with SHRM Labs, 25.2% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners said they have never measured how much recruiter time goes to screening versus strategic work.

What drives cost per hire up or down

Two things move a role up its band, and only one of them shows on an invoice. When talent is scarce, recruiters spend more hours sourcing per hire and teams lean harder on paid channels and agencies 35. And senior or specialized roles need more rounds and longer sourcing cycles, which raises recruiter and hiring-manager time per hire 35.

Hiring Volume, Not Company Size, Sets What You Pay per Hire

Much of your recruiting spend is fixed. The number of hires you divide it across is not. That is the whole mechanic.

A 200-person company making 40 hires on $120,000 of total recruiting spend lands at $3,000 per hire 27. A smaller company spends less overall and still pays more per hire on most role types, because it has no dedicated recruiting team and leans harder on agencies and paid postings. Employers under 50 people often run past $7,500 per hire 36. An enterprise spreads ATS licenses, recruiter salaries and employer branding across hundreds of hires instead of a handful 7. So a low total hiring budget is not evidence of efficient hiring. It usually means a small denominator.

Whether you source in-house or pay agencies is the same volume question. Running an in-house function costs $146,000 to $200,000 a year whether it fills 30 roles or three 37. Agency spend is purely variable, so below roughly 15-20 hires a year the agency is cheaper, and above it the fixed team wins 37. Treat that as a midpoint rather than a threshold: published estimates run from six or seven hires up to 30 or 40 38, mostly because they price different things, one recruiter and a lean tool stack at one end and a full talent acquisition function at the other 39. Your own crossover turns on two questions. Is demand steady enough to keep a recruiter busy through a slow quarter? And are the roles specialized enough that an internal generalist cannot reach the candidates at all?

Flat-rate hiring events push the mechanic furthest, because the fee is fixed per event and the hire count is not 11. Across 582 healthcare hiring events in 2025, employers averaged eight hires each, which works out to under $200 per hire at the top pricing tier before staff time 11. That only holds for multi-role, hourly and clinical hiring where interview volume is the bottleneck.

The Vacancy Cost the Formula Leaves Out: $500 a Day

An empty seat costs money every day it stays empty, and the SHRM/ANSI formula counts none of it 10. For an average professional role, one estimate puts the loss at about $500 a day in output 10. Run that against SHRM's 2026 median time-to-fill of 39 calendar days for non-executive positions and the open seat costs roughly $19,500 before a single recruiting invoice arrives 9. Sales roles can lose $7,000 to $10,000 a month in deals that never close 10, and executive searches run longer still, at a median of 45 days 8. Pressure to fill fast then pushes you toward agencies and sponsored listings, the most expensive channels available 12. A slow process raises both halves at once.

Four things keep your own figure defensible:

  • Match the period on both sides. Annual recruiting spend divided by a quarterly hire count is not a cost per hire. Count hires whole, never as fractional full-time equivalents 6.
  • Segment before you compare. A bulk hiring push averaged with one specialized search misstates both 35.
  • Include every search, not just the wins. All recruiting costs in a period go over all hires in that period, failed searches included.
  • Read cost next to quality of hire. Only 20% of organizations track the two together, and a cheap hire who leaves at month six was never cheap 4.

The first three protect the arithmetic. The fourth protects the decision, because cost falls fastest when the bar drops.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average cost per hire in the US?

The current SHRM benchmark is $5,475 for a non-executive hire, an average from the 2025 Benchmarking Report 1. The US median sits near $1,633, because high-volume entry-level roles pull it down 33.

How do you lower cost per hire without dropping hiring quality?

Cut interview hours before you cut spend. Screen more tightly earlier in the funnel, batch interview panels, and pay for referrals. Referred hires cost 40-60% less than agency placements and stay longer 27.

Does cost per hire include onboarding costs?

No. Under the SHRM standard the count stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding falls outside it 11. Track training and ramp time separately, or the two figures stop being comparable across periods.

What is the difference between cost per hire and time to fill?

Cost per hire measures money spent per filled seat. Time to fill measures the calendar days from open requisition to accepted offer. A slow fill raises cost, because an empty professional seat loses roughly $500 a day 10.

How much does it cost to replace an employee who leaves?

Replacement is a separate metric, and folding it into cost per hire breaks the comparison. Common estimates run from six to nine months of the departing employee's salary, climbing sharply for specialized and senior roles.
