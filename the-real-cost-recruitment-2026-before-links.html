# The Real Cost Per Hire in 2026: $4,700 Is Two Editions Old

You are halfway through a budget review when someone asks what a hire costs you. You give a number. Someone across the table gives a different one from a different deck, and neither of you can say which report it came from.

That is the state of most cost per hire figures in circulation. They have been cut loose from the year, the survey and the method that made them mean anything. And even the well-sourced ones describe less than half of what a hire actually takes out of your business.

Quick answer

Cost per hire is what you spend to source, assess and hire one person, divided by the number of people you hired in the same period. The benchmark most teams still quote has been superseded twice. The current average for a non-executive hire is $5,475 1.

Even that number only counts what somebody sent you an invoice for, and the invoiced side is the smaller side. Most of what a hire costs is interviewer and hiring-manager time that no accounting system records. So build your own figure by role band and by how many hires you make.

The $4,700 Cost Per Hire Figure Is Two SHRM Editions Old

The $4,700 in your budget deck is not wrong, it is answering a question about a hiring market that has moved on. The figure comes from a SHRM article published in April 2022, which reported the average cost of a hire as nearly $4,700 2. It has been repeated in enough blog posts since that it now reads like a permanent fact about hiring, rather than one survey's answer in one year.

The number to plan a 2026 budget against is $5,475 for a non-executive hire, from SHRM's 2025 Benchmarking Report 1. That is the live edition of the same series, so it replaces the older figure rather than competing with it. The gap between them sounds like a rounding error on one requisition. Multiply it across a year of hiring and it is a variance nobody budgeted for.

The follow-up question is the bigger problem. When your CFO asks which report the number came from and what year it covers, "the SHRM number" is not an answer, and the pause while you look it up costs you the rest of the slide. Write the report and the year beside the figure. Four extra words buy you the right to be believed about the rest of the deck.

Why One Report Says $35,879 and Another Says $10,600

Go looking for what an executive hire costs and you can find $35,879 and $10,600 in the same afternoon, both current, both from SHRM. Neither is a mistake, and neither corrects the other. One is an average, from the 2025 Benchmarking Report, and the other is a median, from the 2026 Recruiting Executives brief 3.

That difference decides what each number is good for. An average adds every search together and divides, so a handful of punishing C-suite searches pull it upward even when the rest of the year was ordinary. A median ignores how extreme the outliers are and asks only which search sat in the middle. The average tells you what your year cost, and the median tells you what your next search will probably cost.

The direction those numbers move matters more than either one alone. The executive median has climbed from $5,000 in 2017 to $10,600 in 2025, and the 2026 series puts it at $15,000 3. Whatever senior hiring cost when you last set a budget, it costs more now, and the rise is concentrated at the top of the org chart rather than spread evenly across it.

So the working rule is a boring one. Never set a figure from one series against a figure from the other. If last year's slide used an average, this year's uses an average. Mix them and you present a trend that exists nowhere except in your spreadsheet.

How to Calculate Cost Per Hire, and Where the Count Stops

Underneath every benchmark above sits one short piece of arithmetic. The standard SHRM and ANSI treat as definitive is CPH = (Total Internal Recruiting Costs + Total External Recruiting Costs) / Total Number of Hires 4. Three inputs and one division, and every argument you will have about the result is an argument about what belongs in the first two buckets.

The internal bucket is everything your own organization pays for: recruiter salaries and their loaded overhead, hiring manager and interviewer time, HR administrative time, recruiting technology, and referral bonuses 4. Almost none of it generates a document you can point at, because it is already sitting inside payroll. That is why it is the half that gets quietly left out.

The external bucket is everything you pay somebody else: job board postings, advertising, agency or retained search fees, background checks and assessments, sign-on bonuses, and relocation 4. These arrive with a date, a vendor and an amount attached, so your finance system will hand you most of this list in an afternoon.

Then there is the boundary, which matters more than either list. Under the SHRM standard the count stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding falls outside it 5. Training, ramp time and the cost of a hire who quits in month six are all real costs, and none belong here. Keep them as their own metrics, or you lose the ability to compare one quarter with the next.

Worked through, it is less intimidating than it sounds. Say you made 15 hires last quarter, spent $30,000 internally and $25,000 externally: that is $55,000 across 15 hires, or $3,667 each 4. The arithmetic takes a minute. Agreeing with your CFO on what goes in each bucket is the work.

Hard Costs vs Soft Costs: You Only See 30-40% of the Bill

Only 30 to 40% of what a hire costs ever arrives as an invoice. The rest, roughly 60% of the total, is time department leaders and managers spend screening applications and running interviews 2. So a figure assembled from receipts is not a slightly incomplete picture of your hiring spend. It is the minority of it.

Hard costs are the easy half precisely because somebody billed you for them. The job ads, the assessment tools, the agency fee, the software subscription: each lands in the ledger with a date and an amount, and you can defend every line because a vendor already did the counting.

Soft costs behave nothing like that. A hiring manager who spends six hours in interviews this week costs your company exactly what she cost last week, to the cent. The money moved out of her own work and into hiring, and no system recorded that it moved. The spend is real, the impact is real, and there is no transaction to point at.

This is also why the standard cost-per-hire calculator stops at the invoice. It is built on the fields a finance system can export, and a finance system has no field for an engineer's Tuesday afternoon. So most teams spend their effort on the smaller half of the bill. Trimming a job board contract is a saving, and it is worth less than one panel interview you did not need to run.

A Technical Hire Costs 23 Interview Hours Before Anyone Is Paid

The uninvoiced half of a hire is mostly one thing, and it is hours in interviews. A technical hire averages 23.3 hours of total interview time, against 12.2 hours for a business hire 6. That is close to three working days per engineer, taken from people whose actual job is shipping product, and spread across enough calendars that nobody feels the whole of it.

Price those hours and the number stops being abstract. A recruiter carrying 15 open roles with three interviews per role per round burns more than 45 hours of senior calendar time before a single offer goes out, and at a blended loaded rate of $100 an hour that is $4,500 of interview labor 7. Every input there is a rate you already pay, which is why the figure survives a budget review.

The useful thing about counting hours is that hours have obvious levers. You cannot negotiate your engineers' salaries down, but you can cut a panel from five interviewers to three, batch a round into one afternoon instead of three weeks of scheduling, and decide in advance what each stage tests so two of them stop testing the same thing.

The biggest lever sits earlier than any of that. Every hour of interview time gets committed the moment somebody decides a candidate deserves a panel. If ten people reach the final round and only two ever had a real chance, you paid for eight sets of interviews to learn something a structured screen could have told you in an hour.

Cost Per Hire by Role: $1,500 at Entry Level, $250,000 for a Retained Search

A blended company-wide average is the least useful number in this article, because nobody hires an average. The bands sit two orders of magnitude apart, and the band you are opening a requisition in is the only one that matters to the budget conversation ahead of you.

One line item does most of the work in that spread, and it is the agency fee. On a $120,000 role, the fee alone runs $18,000 to $36,000 depending on the rate you agreed 8. Set that against the benchmarks earlier in this article: one vendor invoice on one mid-senior role is larger than the entire published cost of an average hire.

Junior and high-volume roles are usually filled without an agency at all, so that line never appears. Senior and specialized roles rarely have the option, because the people worth hiring are not reading your careers page. That difference, more than panels or software or advertising, is most of the distance between the top and bottom of the table.

So the honest answer to "what does a hire cost us" is a question back: which role. Quote the band and name what puts a role at the top of it, and your number survives the manager who wanted one headline figure.

Your Hiring Volume Sets the Number, Not Your Company Size

A large share of recruiting spend is fixed, while the number of hires you divide it across is not. That one sentence explains most of the variation between companies that otherwise look identical. Your applicant tracking system, your recruiter's salary and your careers site cost the same whether you fill forty roles or four.

Work it through with the numbers. A 200-person company making 40 hires against $120,000 of total recruiting spend lands at $3,000 per hire 9. Freeze half those requisitions in a slow quarter, keep the same team and tools, and the identical operation now runs at $6,000 per hire, with nobody having done anything worse than before.

This is why smaller companies look expensive on this metric and mostly are not. They are not worse at hiring, they just have a small denominator, so every fixed cost lands harder. A low total recruiting budget is not evidence of efficient hiring, and reading it as one is how teams cut the tooling holding the process together.

The build-or-buy decision runs on the same arithmetic. Below roughly 15 to 20 hires a year, paying agencies per placement works out cheaper, and above that a fixed internal team wins 10. Treat it as a midpoint rather than a hard threshold, because your own crossover depends on whether hiring stays steady enough to keep a recruiter busy through a quiet quarter.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average cost per hire in the US?

The current SHRM benchmark is $5,475 for a non-executive hire, an average from the 2025 Benchmarking Report 1. The US median sits nearer $1,633, because high-volume entry-level hiring pulls it down 11.

How do you lower cost per hire without dropping hiring quality?

Cut interview hours before you cut spend. Screen harder and earlier, batch your panels into one day, and pay for referrals. Referred hires cost 40-60% less than agency placements and stay longer 9.

Does cost per hire include onboarding costs?

No. The count stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding sits outside it 5. Track training and ramp time as their own number, or you lose the ability to compare one quarter with the next.

What is the difference between cost per hire and time to fill?

One measures money per filled seat, the other measures calendar days from open requisition to accepted offer. They move together, because a slow process pushes you toward agencies and sponsored listings, which are the expensive channels.

How much does it cost to replace an employee who leaves?

That is a separate metric, and folding it into cost per hire breaks the comparison. Common estimates run from six to nine months of the departing person's salary, and go higher for senior and specialized roles.
