# The Real Cost Per Hire in 2026: $4,700 Is Two Editions Old

Ask three executives what a hire costs and you get three different numbers. Every cost per hire figure comes with a report, a year and a method, and most in circulation have lost all three. The $4,700 in your budget deck describes a hiring market that is gone.

The invoices also tell you less than half the story. Roughly 60% of what a hire costs is manager and interviewer time that no accounting system records.

Quick answer

Cost per hire is what you spend to source, assess and hire one person, divided by the hires you made in that period. The benchmark most teams quote is out of date. The current SHRM average is $5,475 for a non-executive hire. Even that counts only what someone billed you for. The screening and interview hours behind a hire cost more. Build your own figure by role band and by hiring volume, and name the report and year behind every number you quote.

The $4,700 Cost Per Hire Is Two SHRM Editions Old

Plan your 2026 budget against $5,475. That is the current SHRM average for a non-executive hire, from the 2025 Benchmarking Report 1. The next edition is due around October 2026 1.

The $4,700 everyone still quotes is not wrong. It is expired. SHRM published it in April 2022, from a survey that closed in November 2021 23. SHRM's own 2025 release says the new study updates that 2022 work 4.

Current SHRM figures can look like they contradict each other too. They do not. Some are averages, some are medians.

The executive median climbed from $5,000 in 2017 to $15,000 in 2026 7. Non-executive costs barely moved 8. That flatness is why a stale figure survived so long.

How to Calculate Cost Per Hire, and Where It Stops

Cost per hire is the total internal and external spend to source, attract, assess and hire one new employee, divided by the hires in that period 9. SHRM and ANSI treat one formula as the standard:

Cost per hire = (Total internal recruiting costs + Total external recruiting costs) / Total number of hires 9

Internal is what you pay your own people and systems: recruiter pay and overhead, hiring manager and interviewer time, HR admin time, recruiting technology, referral bonuses 9.

External is what you pay someone else: job board postings, advertising, agency or retained search fees, background checks and assessments, sign-on bonuses, relocation 9.

The boundary matters more than either list. Under the SHRM standard, counting stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding sits outside it 10.

One quarter, worked: 15 hires, $30,000 internal, $25,000 external. That is $55,000 over 15 hires, or $3,667 each 9.

Hard Costs vs Soft Costs: 30-40% Is All You See

Only 30-40% of what a hire costs arrives as an invoice. The rest, about 60%, is the time your managers and department leaders spend screening and interviewing 2. A figure built from receipts misses well over half the spend 11.

Hard costs are easy to name because someone billed you: job ads, agency fees, assessment tools, recruiting software, relocation, payroll taxes. Soft costs hide inside salaries you already pay, so no separate charge ever appears 12. A manager who spends six hours interviewing this week costs what they cost last week. The money moved. Nothing recorded it.

That is why most calculators stop at the invoice. A finance system has no field for an engineer's afternoon. The difference is visibility, not importance 13.

Agency Fees Run 15-25% of Salary, 40-50% at C-Level

Agency fees are the largest external cost for professional and senior roles, and they scale with salary 5. Expect 15-25% of first-year base at manager through VP level 18, stretching to 30% 19.

On a $120,000 role, the fee alone is $18,000 to $36,000 20. That single line beats most published cost-per-hire benchmarks for a whole hire.

Contingency is the mid-level default: you pay only when a placement happens, and you can run several firms 21. Retained search is exclusive, paid in stages, priced near 30-35% 22. C-level work reaches 40-50% of first-year income 23.

Referrals are the cheap alternative. Referred hires cost 40-60% less than agency placements and stay longer 24, and bonuses run $500 to $5,000 24. Pay within 30 days of the start date and keep the nomination to one form 25.

Soft Costs: 23 Hours of Interviews per Hire

Two soft costs dominate, and both are hours.

The first is interview time. A technical hire takes 23.3 hours of interview time against 12.2 for a business hire 26. Data roles run highest at 24.9 hours, customer support lowest at 8.9 26. Technical roles now average 17.6 interviews per hire, up 52% since 2021 27.

Price those hours and they turn into money. Run 15 open roles with three interviews per round and you burn over 45 hours of senior calendar time before one offer goes out. At $100 an hour loaded, that is $4,500 in interview labor 25. Batching interviews into single scheduled events is the main lever you have 10.

The second is volume. Recruiters now handle about 291 applications per hire, up from 100 in early 2021 26, and workloads rose 93% in a year 28. Nobody added headcount to absorb it. Every hour spent on a bigger pile is an hour not spent closing a finalist.

Hiring Volume, Not Company Size, Sets What You Pay per Hire

Much of your recruiting spend is fixed. The hire count you divide it by is not. That is the whole mechanic.

A 200-person company making 40 hires on $120,000 of recruiting spend pays $3,000 each 24. An employer with fewer than 50 people often runs past $7,500 31. They have no recruiting team, so they lean on agencies and paid postings. An enterprise spreads ATS licenses and recruiter salaries across hundreds of hires 5.

A low total budget is not proof of efficient hiring. It usually means a small denominator.

In-house versus agency is the same question. An internal function costs $146,000 to $200,000 a year whether it fills 30 roles or three 32. Below roughly 15-20 hires a year, agencies win 32. Published crossover points run from six up to 40, mostly because each one prices a different kind of team 3334.

The Vacancy Cost SHRM's Formula Leaves Out: $500 a Day

An empty seat costs you money every day, and the standard formula counts none of it 9. For an average professional role, that is about $500 a day in lost output 9. SHRM's 2026 median time to fill is 39 days for non-executive roles 8. So the seat costs roughly $19,500 before a single invoice lands.

Sales roles lose more, $7,000 to $10,000 a month in deals nobody closes 9. Pressure to fill fast pushes you toward agencies and sponsored listings, the priciest channels there are 11. A slow process raises both halves at once.

Four habits keep your figure defensible:

  • Match the period on both sides. Annual spend over a quarterly hire count is not a cost per hire. Count hires whole 7.
  • Segment before you compare. Averaging a bulk hiring push with one specialized search misstates both 30.
  • Count the searches that failed. All costs in a period go over all hires in that period.
  • Read it next to quality of hire. Only 20% of organizations track both, and a hire who quits at month six was never cheap 4.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average cost per hire in the US?

The current SHRM benchmark is $5,475 for a non-executive hire, an average from the 2025 Benchmarking Report 1. The U.S. median sits near $1,633, because high-volume entry-level roles pull it down 29.

How do you lower cost per hire without dropping hiring quality?

Cut interview hours before you cut spend. Screen harder early, batch your panels, and pay for referrals. Referred hires cost 40-60% less than agency placements and stay longer 24.

Does cost per hire include onboarding costs?

No. Under the SHRM standard, counting stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding sits outside it 10. Track training and ramp time separately, or the two figures stop comparing across periods.

What is the difference between cost per hire and time to fill?

Cost per hire measures money per filled seat. Time to fill measures days from open requisition to accepted offer. A slow fill raises cost: an empty professional seat loses about $500 a day 9.

How much does it cost to replace an employee who leaves?

Replacement is a separate metric, and folding it into cost per hire breaks the comparison. Common estimates run from six to nine months of the leaver's salary, and climb for senior roles.
