# The Real Cost Per Hire in 2026: The 2016 Figure Everyone Quotes

Most hiring budgets still quote a cost per hire of $4,129, a figure from a survey fielded in spring 2016 1. The Society for Human Resource Management (SHRM) now puts a nonexecutive hire near $5,475 on its 2025 average series and an executive hire at a $15,000 median on its 2026 series 23. A 2026 budget still quoting the old $8,800 executive median understates it by about 70% of itself 4. Even the current figures only cover the invoiced share of hiring. That share is an estimated 30-40% of what a hire costs 5. A number you can put in front of a CFO carries its edition, its method, and the costs it leaves out.

The $4,129 Figure Was Collected in Spring 2016

The $4,700 figure that replaced $4,129 belongs to a cycle SHRM now describes as superseded. Both are still quoted in 2026 as if they were current. Neither is.

Take the older one first. SHRM published $4,129 in its Human Capital Benchmarking Report in August 2016 1. It came from 2,048 members and reflected fiscal year 2015 1. So a number circulating in hiring budgets today describes hiring conditions from a decade ago.

The $4,700 figure is younger but not current. SHRM published it on April 11, 2022, in an article titled "The Real Costs of Recruitment" 6. That article names it as an average, but gives no report title, edition year, field dates, or sample size 6. The benchmarking cycle behind it drew on responses collected from April through November 2021 from more than 2,400 members 7. In October 2025 SHRM wrote that its new study updates SHRM's prior benchmarking studies from 2022 8.

The error is quoting any cost of hire figure stripped of its edition, averaging method, and sample. SHRM notes that respondents are not required to answer every metric, so the sample behind a single reported figure varies inside one edition 1. Ask which release, which method, and how many organizations answered that specific question. A figure that cannot survive those three questions cannot anchor a hiring budget.

Why SHRM's Average Is $5,475 and Its Median $15,000

The current SHRM answer depends on which series you are using. SHRM's 2025 benchmarking release reports averages: $5,475 for a nonexecutive hire and $35,879 for an executive one, with executives costing nearly seven times more 2. The 2026 recruiting brief reports medians instead, which is why its executive series reads $15,000 rather than $35,879 3. SHRM says medians avoid being skewed by very high or very low values and improve comparability across time periods.

The samples moved too. The 2025 survey ran electronically from January 9 to March 3, 2025, oversampled vice presidents and above, and drew 2,371 respondents 8. The 2026 report drew on 4,657 members, oversampled director-level and above, and began collecting data on November 24, 2025 3. So the comparison rule that survives scrutiny is narrow: compare averages only to averages, medians only to medians, and only within the same published series.

How to Calculate Cost Per Hire, and Where It Stops

Cost per hire is the total spend to source, attract, and assess one new employee. SHRM treats one formula as the standard: CPH = (Total Internal Recruiting Costs + Total External Recruiting Costs) ÷ Total Number of Hires 9. Divide everything spent in a period by every hire made in that same period. Two buckets feed it, which is why two honest finance teams can produce different numbers from the same formula.

Internal costs are the ones your own organization absorbs:

  • Recruiter pay and overhead: salaries, benefits, taxes, and allocated overhead for the in-house talent acquisition (TA) function 9.
  • Hiring manager and interviewer time: panel hours valued at a loaded hourly rate, including debriefs 10. It is the line most often understated 9.
  • HR administrative time: coordinator scheduling and system updates 9.
  • Recruiting technology: the Applicant Tracking System (ATS), sourcing tools, and assessment platforms 9.
  • Referral bonuses and recruiting events: internal referral payouts, job fairs, and campus recruiting 9.

External costs are the invoices from outside vendors:

  • Job board postings and advertising: per-posting and subscription fees, plus employer brand campaigns 9.
  • Agency and retained search fees: typically 20-30% of first-year salary for contingency work, and 30-35% for retained executive search 9.
  • Background checks and assessments: roughly $20-$100 per screening check and $30-$200 per candidate assessed 9.
  • Sign-on bonuses and relocation: counted when they are required to close the hire 9.

Then the boundary: under the SHRM standard, the count stops at the accepted offer, and onboarding sits outside it. Vacancy cost and ramp cost are excluded too 9. That line matters because the next costs are still hiring costs, just not costs inside the benchmark.

Hard Costs Are Only 30-40% of What a Hire Costs

The SHRM formula defines the benchmark. It does not define the whole hiring bill. Hard costs run 30-40% of total hiring spend, and time-based soft costs make up the remaining 60-70% 511. That ratio is the reason a benchmark figure and a hiring budget rarely match.

Hard costs show up on invoices and credit card statements. They include job ad spend, agency and search-firm fees, screening and assessment tools, recruiting software, relocation packages, and employer payroll taxes. Finance can track them because each line has a price.

The soft side is where the money hides. It covers recruiter and hiring manager hours, employee interview time, productivity lost while a seat sits empty, ramp-up, training, knowledge transfer, and the damage a poor hire does to a team. Edie Goldberg, SHRM Foundation chair-elect and co-author of The Inside Gig, put the split at "30 percent to 40 percent are hard costs, and the other 60 percent are soft costs" 6. That is an expert estimate from a 2022 interview, not a figure measured from SHRM survey data 6. Treat it as a planning ratio, not a benchmark.

Follow the ratio out and the total stops looking like the headline. Most cost-per-hire calculators will not return the loaded number, because they stop at the invoices.

Standard calculators inherit the boundary of the formula they run. They price what the finance system already captured. They omit manager time leakage, tool-stack bloat, no-show interviews, and the productivity cost of an unfilled seat 12. Recruiter hours at least get discussed; hiring manager and panel hours usually go untracked entirely.

So carry two lines in the budget, not one. The benchmark buys you the invoiced 30-40%. Everything the rest of this article prices sits in the other 60-70%.

Agency Fees: 20-35% of Salary, 40-50% for C-Suite

Agency and recruiter fees are the largest external cost item for organizations filling professional or senior roles 13. They are charged as a percentage of first-year salary, so the line item scales with seniority instead of staying fixed.

Three fee structures cover almost every engagement. Contingency search, the common arrangement for mid-level roles, runs 20-30% of first-year salary and is paid only when a candidate accepts 14. You can run several contingency firms at once and pay only the one that places. The tradeoff is that each firm gives your search less dedicated time and reaches fewer passive candidates 15.

Retained search costs around 30-35% of salary, sometimes plus expenses, paid in installments as search stages complete 16. It buys exclusivity and the firm's sustained attention on one client 17. Hybrid deals, sometimes called container or retingency arrangements, take 10-25% of the total up front and the balance on placement 18.

Treat the 20-35% band as a starting point rather than a list price. Seniority and candidate-pool size move the number inside it 19. Recruiters on both sides of the table will tell you the quoted rate is often negotiated down.

The percentage climbs with the role, and so does the base it applies to. Many organizations skip agencies entirely for junior roles. At manager through VP level, permanent placement fees commonly run 20-30% of base salary 20. C-level searches carry the highest fees in the market, reaching 40-50% of first-year income at the top end 18.

Watch the dollar figure, not the percentage. A $120,000 engineering hire costs $24,000 to $36,000 in agency fees 21. For C-suite and board searches, some firms set hard floors regardless of pay, starting at $75,000 22. Executive engagements also add administrative fees of 10-15%, plus travel, background checks, and advertising the vacancy, which can add another 5-15% to the total 18.

Technical Hires Cost 23.3 Hours of Interview Time

The largest soft cost inside the benchmark's boundary is interview time. A technical hire consumes 23.3 hours of total interview time, against 12.2 hours for a business hire 23. That is more than 11 extra hours from engineers and managers who are not recruiters. None of it appears on an invoice, which is why it rarely reaches the cost-per-hire number.

The load has grown. Technical roles now average 17.6 interviews per hire, up 52% from roughly 11 in 2021 2425. Business roles average 11.7, up 36% 2425. Broken out by function, Data roles top the chart at 24.9 hours per hire. Engineering sits at 24.7, and Customer Support at the bottom takes 8.9 26. That is a more than threefold spread 26.

The variance matters as much as the average. A budget built on the mean will understate a specialized engineering search. One founder's accounting of a senior engineering loop puts engineering time at roughly $15,000 per hire versus about $4,000 of recruiting time 27. The difference comes from counting the cost of interrupting an engineer 27.

Batching interviews into one scheduled event is the largest available lever on this cost in high-volume hiring. Spreading them across weeks of calendar back-and-forth keeps the same interviews but adds delay.

The Vacancy and Ramp Costs SHRM Leaves Out

The SHRM/ANSI standard stops at the accepted offer, so two of the largest hiring costs sit outside the benchmark: the empty seat and the months before the new person is fully productive 9. Neither shows up on an invoice. Both are money.

Start with the vacancy. Deloitte's recruitment efficiency research puts an unfilled average professional role at about $500 per day in lost productivity 28. The figure rises for specialized or revenue-generating positions 28. Against a median time to fill of 63.5 days in 2026, down from 67.7 days in 2025, that daily figure compounds fast 9. At $500 a day across 44 working days, a single standard vacancy costs $22,000 before a recruiter spends anything 9.

Revenue-generating roles run higher. A senior sales seat at $2,500 a day over 45 days reaches $112,500 28.

Ramp time is harder to pin down, and honesty here matters more than precision. Published onboarding costs vary by roughly three to five times depending on what each source counts. One benchmark puts average U.S. onboarding at $1,500 to $5,000 per employee, covering training, mentoring, and initial HR processing 29.

The drag underneath that lasts longer than the invoice suggests. Research referenced by the Association for Talent Development finds a professional hire reaches full productivity between three and nine months after starting 13. Full salary is paid throughout for partial output 13. Leaving that out understates the cost to hire by a wide margin 13.

Stack the three together and the benchmark looks small. A non-executive hire at the $5,475 SHRM average, plus $22,000 of vacancy, plus onboarding at the top of its published band, puts the loaded cost near six times the headline figure 30929. The number you defend to a CFO should say which of those three layers it includes.

In-House vs Agency: The 15-20 Hires a Year Breakeven

Hiring volume moves cost per hire as much as role type does. Part of recruiting spend is fixed, and the hire count determines how thinly that fixed cost gets spread. A 40-person company filling eight roles in a quarter with $6,000 in external costs and $9,000 in internal costs lands at $1,875 per hire 12. A larger organization spending $100,000 a year on HR salaries and hiring tools plus $75,000 on external recruiting services, across 50 hires, comes to $3,500 36.

That first figure is not a sign the smaller company runs a better function. It is filling neither executive nor specialized technical roles at the rate a large enterprise does 12. Smaller organizations usually face higher costs per hire while spending less in total. They lack scale, have no dedicated recruiting team, and lean harder on agencies or job board postings.

When you make five to 10 hires a year rather than 500, every hiring decision carries disproportionate weight on the budget 30. An enterprise spreads ATS licenses, recruiter salaries, and employer branding across hundreds or thousands of hires 30.

One channel reverses the arithmetic. Flat-rate hiring events cost the same whether they produce four hires or 40, so cost per hire falls as volume rises. The caveat is narrow: it works only for multi-role, hourly, and clinical hiring where interview volume is the bottleneck.

Only 20% Pair Cost Per Hire With Quality of Hire

Cost per hire only survives scrutiny when it is paired with quality of hire. Only 20% of organizations track the two together 30. A figure that falls while ninety-day retention falls with it is not a saving, it is a deferred cost 37. Report the two together, or expect the attrition to arrive in the next quarter's data anyway 37.

Four rules keep the arithmetic honest. Match the period on both sides: annual costs divided by a monthly hire count distorts the result 12. Count whole hires, not fractional full-time equivalents 12. Segment by role level, department, and hiring type before you compare anything, because a bulk hiring push averaged against one specialized search misstates both 10. Resist cross-company benchmarking outright, since industry, role mix, geography, and methodology all move the figure 9.

That last rule is the one most often broken. Benchmark yourself against your own prior periods first. Most teams will tell you the outside number was never the argument that landed.

Frequently asked questions

How do I reduce cost per hire?

Batch interviews into a single scheduled event, the largest available lever in high-volume hiring. Then match your sourcing model to your volume: under 15-20 hires a year, agencies cost less than an in-house team 38. Report either move alongside quality of hire, because a figure that falls while ninety-day retention falls with it is a deferred cost, not a saving 37.

What is the difference between cost per hire and time to fill?

Cost per hire measures money spent per hire. Time to fill measures days from open role to accepted offer, a median of 63.5 days in 2026 9. Each vacant day costs roughly $500 in lost productivity 28.

Should internal promotions count in cost per hire?

The SHRM formula divides recruiting costs by total hires in the period 9. Whether internal moves count is a definition your team must fix and hold. Report internal fills separately: they cost far less and pull the blended figure down.

What is recruitment cost ratio?

Recruitment cost ratio is total recruiting spend divided by the hire's first-year compensation, shown as a percentage. It scales with salary, so it compares an executive search to an entry-level fill more fairly than a flat dollar figure.

How often should you recalculate cost per hire?

Quarterly, on a rolling 12 months, so one expensive search does not distort a short window. Recalculate sooner if your role mix or hiring volume changes, because both move the figure more than recruiting efficiency does.
