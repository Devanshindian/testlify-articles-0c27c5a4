# The Real Cost Per Hire in 2026: $4,700 Is Two Editions Old

Ask three executives what a hire costs and you will get three different numbers, each quoted with total confidence. Every cost per hire figure carries a report, a year and a method behind it, and most of the ones circulating in budget decks have lost all three. The number you are about to defend in a planning meeting may describe a hiring market that closed years ago.

Then there is the half nobody invoices. Most of what a hire costs is your own people's time, spent reading applications and sitting in interviews, and no accounting system records a minute of it. This piece gives you a figure you can source, split into both halves, and read by role.

Quick answer

Cost per hire is what you spend to source, assess and hire one person, divided by the hires you made in the same period. The benchmark most teams still quote comes from a superseded SHRM cycle. The current figure for a non-executive hire is $5,475 1. Even that counts only what somebody billed you for, and the screening and interview hours behind a hire cost more than the invoices do. Build your own number by role band, and name the report and the year behind every figure you quote.

The $4,700 Cost Per Hire Is Two SHRM Editions Old

The current benchmark for a non-executive hire is $5,475, from SHRM's 2025 Benchmarking Report 1. That is the figure to plan a 2026 budget against. SHRM refreshes the series once a year in the fourth quarter, so nothing newer is due until around October 2026 1.

The $4,700 that still anchors most budget decks is not wrong so much as expired. It comes from a SHRM article published in April 2022, which reported the average cost per hire as nearly $4,700 2. Four years is a long time in recruiting. Anyone quoting it today is describing a labour market that has since been through a hiring surge, a freeze and a round of layoffs.

This matters in the room, not on the page. A finance director asks where your number came from, you say SHRM, and the meeting moves on. Then someone checks, finds a report two editions old, and every other figure in your deck gets read with the same suspicion.

The fix costs nothing. Name the report, the year, and whether the figure is an average or a median, every time you use it.

Why SHRM Reports $35,879 and $10,600 for the Same Hire

Look up what an executive hire costs and you will find two SHRM figures that appear to contradict each other. One says $35,879. The other says $10,600. Both are real, both are current, and neither is a correction of the other.

The difference is the statistic, not the data. The first is an average from the 2025 Benchmarking Report; the second is a median from the 2026 Recruiting Executives brief 3. An average adds every search together and divides by the count, so the handful of enormous searches at the top drag it well above what a typical search costs. A median ignores how big those outliers are and reports the middle search instead.

So pick one series and stay inside it. If your board deck tracks medians, every year in that chart has to be a median from the same report. Mix the two and you will end up explaining a cost increase that never happened.

How to Calculate Cost Per Hire, and Where It Stops

Cost per hire is the total internal and external spend needed to source, attract, assess and hire one new employee, divided by the number of hires made in the same period 4. SHRM and ANSI treat one formula as the standard, and it is short: total internal recruiting costs plus total external recruiting costs, divided by total hires 4. Everything hard about this metric is in deciding what belongs on the top line.

The internal side is everything your own organization pays for anyway. That means recruiter salaries and the overhead loaded onto them, hiring manager and interviewer time, HR administrative time, recruiting technology, and referral bonuses 4. None of it arrives as a separate bill, so you are counting a share of what you already spend on people already on payroll.

The external side is everything you pay somebody else. That means job board postings, advertising, agency or retained search fees, background checks and assessments, sign-on bonuses, and relocation 4. This half is the easy one, because your finance system already holds it. Export the recruiting cost centre for the quarter and most of these lines are sitting there with a date and an amount.

Then decide where you stop counting, and write the decision down. Under the SHRM standard the count ends at the accepted offer, so onboarding and ramp time sit outside it 5. A recruiter who quietly folds three weeks of training into one quarter and not the next has produced two numbers that cannot be compared. Nobody on the leadership team will spot it.

Hard Costs vs Soft Costs: 30-40% Is All You See

One number should change how you build your own figure. Only 30-40% of what a hire costs arrives as an invoice, and the rest, roughly 60%, is time: the hours department leaders and managers spend screening applications and sitting in interviews 2. A cost per hire assembled from receipts is missing more than half the bill.

Hard costs are easy to name because somebody billed you for them. Job ad spend, agency fees, assessment tools, an applicant tracking system subscription, a relocation package. Each lands in the general ledger with a date and an amount, which is why every calculator online stops there.

Soft costs behave differently, and the difference is visibility rather than size. A hiring manager who spends six hours in interviews this week costs your company exactly what she cost last week, because her salary does not move. The money moved anyway, out of the work she would otherwise have done. So run the figure twice, once from the invoices and once with those hours priced at a loaded hourly rate, and quote the second one.

A technical hire now costs 23 interview hours before anyone is paid

Interview time is the largest single hidden cost in most hiring budgets, and it scales with the role. A technical hire averages 23.3 hours of total interview time, against 12.2 hours for a business hire 6. That is most of a working week of senior calendar time, spent filling one seat.

Those hours become money the moment you price them. Picture a recruiter carrying 15 open roles, with three interviews per role per round. That is more than 45 hours of interviewer time gone before a single offer letter is written, and at a blended loaded rate of $100 an hour it comes to $4,500 of interview labour across those searches 7.

Price it that way and nobody argues with you, because every input is a rate your finance team already signed off on. Put that $4,500 beside your invoiced recruiting spend for the same quarter and the argument about where hiring money goes changes shape.

The lever here is your calendar, not your headcount. A panel that meets a candidate in one batched session spends fewer hours in total than the same panel spread across three weeks, because most of the waste is in the rescheduling rather than the interviewing.

Applications per hire tripled, and recruiter workload rose 93% in a year

The second uninvoiced cost is volume, and almost nobody added headcount to absorb it. Recruiters now process roughly 291 applications per hire, up from about 100 in early 2021 6, while recruiter workloads climbed 93% year over year 8. Talk to any recruiter and you hear the same complaint: much of that inbound is duplicated or auto-generated.

That triage time has to come out of something, and it comes out of the work that lowers cost per hire. An hour spent sorting a bigger pile is an hour not spent selling the role to a strong candidate, or closing a finalist who is holding two other offers. The pile grows, the close rate slips, and an agency invoice eventually arrives to fix it.

Most teams have never checked how bad this has become. In our topline report with SHRM Labs, 25.2% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners said they have never measured how much recruiter time goes to screening versus strategic work. Start there. One week of honest time tracking tells you whether your screening step is a filter or a bottleneck.

Agency Fees Run 15-25% of Salary, 40-50% at C-Level

Agency fees are where an ordinary hire turns into an expensive one, and they do not sit at a fixed price. The published band runs 15-25% of first-year base salary for manager through VP roles 9, and quoted deals cluster near the top of it. Recruiters will tell you, politely, that a fee agreed before a search starts is not reopened once a candidate says yes.

Put that on a salary and the scale is obvious. On a $120,000 role the agency fee alone runs $18,000 to $36,000 10. That single line is larger than most published cost-per-hire benchmarks for an entire hire, and it explains almost all of the distance between what an entry-level hire costs you and what an executive one does.

The question this forces is not whether agencies are worth the money. It is which roles need one. Junior and mid-level roles are often filled direct, so the fee never enters the calculation at all. Senior and specialized roles rarely have that option.

Cost Per Hire by Role: $1,500 Entry to $250,000 Retained

Both halves of the bill scale with the role, which is why a blended company-wide average tells you almost nothing about the requisition you are about to open. An entry-level hire and a retained executive search sit two orders of magnitude apart. The table below gives the 2026 benchmark by band 4.

Mid-level professional$3,500-$8,000Standard interview loops, a mix of agency and direct hires

Executive, retained search$50,000-$250,000Retained fees charged on first-year total compensation

Read the spread rather than the midpoint. The U.S. median cost per hire sits near $1,633, well under any blended average, because high-volume entry-level roles pull the number down while executive searches pull it up 11. If your own average is climbing, check your hiring mix before you check your process. Three senior hires in one quarter move it further than anything your recruiters did.

Company size moves the number too, but not in the way people assume. A large share of recruiting spend is fixed, and the number of hires you divide it across is not. An enterprise spreads its ATS licences, recruiter salaries and employer branding across hundreds of hires, while a fifteen-person company spreads the same kinds of cost across three.

That is why micro-employers with fewer than 50 employees often run past $7,500 per hire 12. A low total hiring budget is not evidence of efficient hiring. It is usually evidence of a small denominator.

What actually widens the gap between bands

Two things push a role up its band, and only one of them ever shows up on an invoice.

The first is specialization. When the talent is scarce your recruiters spend far more hours sourcing each hire, and the team leans harder on paid channels and agencies to reach people who are not looking 13. Both effects raise what the hire costs, but only the agency fee is visible to finance, so that is the one that gets questioned.

The second is interview intensity. Senior and specialized roles need more rounds and longer sourcing cycles, which raises recruiter and hiring-manager time per hire 13. This is the half you can actually act on. Cutting one round out of a five-round loop takes hours out of every search you run this year, and trying it costs you nothing.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average cost per hire in the US?

The current SHRM benchmark is $5,475 for a non-executive hire, an average from the 2025 Benchmarking Report 1. The U.S. median sits near $1,633, because high-volume entry-level roles pull it down 11.

How do you lower cost per hire without dropping hiring quality?

Cut interview hours before you cut spend. Screen candidates on job-related skills earlier in the funnel, and batch your panel into one scheduled session. The largest line in the bill shrinks without anyone lowering the bar.

Does cost per hire include onboarding costs?

No. Under the SHRM standard the count stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding falls outside it 5. Track training and ramp time separately, or the two figures stop being comparable across periods.

What is the difference between cost per hire and time to fill?

Cost per hire measures money spent per filled seat. Time to fill measures the calendar days from an open requisition to an accepted offer. A slow fill raises the cost, because your own people spend more hours on it.

How much does it cost to replace an employee who leaves?

Replacement is a separate metric, and folding it into cost per hire breaks the comparison. Common estimates run from six to nine months of the departing employee's salary, and climb for specialized roles.
