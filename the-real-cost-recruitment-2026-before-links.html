# The Real Cost Per Hire in 2026: $4,700 Is Two Editions Old

Ask three executives what a hire costs and you get three numbers, each said with confidence. Every figure comes from a report, a year and a method, and most of the ones in circulation have lost all three. The $4,700 still anchoring budget decks describes a hiring market that no longer exists.

The invoices tell you less than half the story anyway. Roughly 60% of a hire's cost is interviewer and manager time that no accounting system records. The number you can defend is the current one: sourced, split into both halves, and read by role and by hiring volume.

Quick answer

Cost per hire is what you spend to source, assess and hire one person, divided by the hires made in the same period. The benchmark most teams quote comes from a dead SHRM cycle. The current one is $5,475 for a non-executive hire. Current releases also mix averages with medians, so figures for the same year can look like they contradict each other. Either way, the figure only covers what someone billed you for. The screening and interview hours behind a hire cost more than the invoices do. Build the number by role band and by hiring volume, and name the report and year behind every figure you quote.

The $4,700 Cost Per Hire Is Two SHRM Editions Old

The current benchmark for a non-executive hire is $5,475, from SHRM's 2025 Benchmarking Report released October 15, 2025 1. That is the number to budget 2026 against.

The $4,700 everyone still quotes is not wrong so much as expired. It comes from a SHRM article published April 11, 2022 2, built on a survey that ran from April to November 2021 3. SHRM's 2025 release says plainly that it updates that 2022 work 4.

Older still is the $4,129 you also see quoted, from a report covering fiscal year 2015 5. So the two most common numbers in recruiting content are not one stale statistic. They are two dead cycles, a decade apart, both passed off as live.

Quote a figure without its edition and method and you cannot defend it when someone asks where it came from. Even the current numbers look like they disagree, because SHRM publishes more than one and they measure different things.

Why SHRM Reports $35,879 and $10,600 for the Same Hire

Both figures are current SHRM numbers. One is an average from the 2025 Benchmarking Report, the other a median from the 2026 Recruiting Executives brief 6. They measure different things about the same tier of hire, so setting one against the other invents a contradiction.

The executive number tripled while everyone else's went flat

The executive median climbed from $5,000 in 2017 to $10,600 in 2025, then to $15,000 in 2026 6. Non-executive costs barely moved across the same series 9. SHRM puts that flatness down to a cooling job market rather than any change in method.

That is why an old figure survived so long unchallenged. For most roles a recruiting team fills, the number had not moved. An article citing the SHRM benchmark in 2023 was not obviously wrong then. It is wrong now, and at the executive tier by a wide margin.

How to Calculate Cost Per Hire, and Where It Stops

One definition sits behind every figure above. Cost per hire is everything you spend, inside and outside, to source, attract, assess and hire one person, divided by the hires made in the same period 10. SHRM and ANSI write it as: (internal recruiting costs + external recruiting costs) / total hires 10.

Internal is what you pay your own side: recruiter salaries and overhead, hiring manager and interviewer time, HR admin time, recruiting software, referral bonuses 10. External is what you pay everyone else: job ads, agency or search fees, background checks and assessments, sign-on bonuses, relocation 10.

The boundary matters more than either list. The count stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding sits outside it 11.

One quarter, worked: 15 hires, $30,000 internal, $25,000 external. That is $55,000 over 15 hires, or $3,667 each 10.

Hard Costs vs Soft Costs: 30-40% Is All You See

Only 30-40% of what a hire costs arrives as an invoice. The rest, roughly 60%, is time: the hours managers and department leaders spend screening applications and running interviews 2. Build the figure from receipts and you have captured well under half the spend 12.

Hard costs are easy to name because someone billed you for them. Job ads, agency fees, assessment tools, recruiting software, relocation and payroll taxes all land in the ledger with a date and an amount.

Soft costs hide inside salaries and calendars you already pay for, so no separate charge ever appears 13. A manager who spends six hours on interviews this week costs exactly what they cost last week. The money moved, and nothing recorded that it did.

That is why standard calculators stop at the invoice. They run on the fields a finance system can export, and finance has no field for an engineer's afternoon. The difference is visibility, not importance 14. Work only from the invoice and you spend your time optimizing the smaller half of the bill.

Ad Spend, Checks and ATS: What Each Line Costs

Before agency fees enter the picture, the invoiced side of a hire is four lines you can price today.

  • Job advertising: LinkedIn contract postings run $200-$1,000 a month per slot 12. Posting to three boards at once puts a business at $800-$1,500 a month 15.
  • Background checks and assessments: Basic screening runs $20-$40, and a full check passes $100 12. Verifications are charged per employer and criminal searches per county, so someone who has moved four times in seven years costs several times more to screen 16. Skills assessments add about $50 a candidate 17.
  • ATS subscription: An applicant tracking system (the software that stores and sorts your applicants) runs $250-$3,000 a month depending on platform and team size 18. Spread it across the hires you make in the same period, never a longer one.
  • Relocation and signing bonuses: A domestic package averages $21,792 for a renter and $63,685 for a homeowner 12. Reimbursements count as taxable wages, so a gross-up adds about 42% on top 19.

One warning on the advertising line. You pay per click, not per hire, so the same money buys a different number of hires each quarter. It is the least predictable input in an otherwise predictable half of the budget.

Agency Fees Run 15-25% of Salary, 40-50% at C-Level

Agency fees are the biggest external cost for professional and senior roles, and they scale with salary instead of sitting at a fixed price 7. The published band is 15-25% of first-year base salary from manager through VP 20, stretched to 30% in other benchmarks 21. Quoted deals cluster at the top of that range. A fee agreed before the search starts is not up for renegotiation once you pick someone.

On a $120,000 role, the agency fee alone runs $18,000 to $36,000 22. That single line is bigger than most published cost-per-hire benchmarks for an entire hire. It explains most of the distance between an entry-level number and an executive one.

Junior roles often skip the agency entirely. Senior and specialized roles rarely can.

Contingency, retained or container: what you are buying with each

Contingency is the mid-level default: you pay only if a placement happens, and you can run several firms at once 23. The trade is less dedicated attention and weaker reach into people who are not job hunting 23. Retained search is exclusive, paid in installments as stages complete, at roughly 30-35% of salary 24. Container splits the difference, part upfront to buy recruiter time and the rest due on placement 25.

C-level searches top the market at 40-50% of first-year income 26, with admin fees and search expenses added on top 26. Budget the fee as a range, not a rate.

A $2,000 referral bonus against an $18,000 placement fee

Referral bonuses usually land between $500 and $5,000 per hire 27. Set that against the $18,000 an agency charges on the $120,000 role above 22. Referred hires also cost 40-60% less than agency placements and stay longer 27.

These programs fail on process, not money. Pay the bonus within 30 days of the start date and keep the nomination to one form 28.

Soft Costs: 23 Hours of Interviews per Hire

That is the last of the invoiced lines. The rest of a hire is time, and it is the larger half. Indirect costs never reach an invoice but still land on the bottom line 14. They are interviewer and manager hours, output lost while a seat sits empty, ramp time, and the fallout when a hire does not work out 14. Only the first sits inside cost per hire, because the count stops at the accepted offer.

Two of those costs dominate, and both are hours.

A technical hire costs 23 interview hours before anyone is paid

Interview time is the largest hidden cost in most hiring budgets, and it scales with the role. A technical hire averages 23.3 hours of interview time against 12.2 for a business hire 29. Data roles run highest at 24.9 hours, customer support lowest at 8.9 29.

Price those hours and they turn into money. A recruiter running 15 open roles with three interviews per role per round burns over 45 hours of senior calendar time before a single offer goes out. At a loaded $100 an hour, that is $4,500 in interview labor 28. It survives a budget review because every input is a rate you already pay.

Technical roles now average 17.6 interviews per hire, up 52% from roughly 11 in 2021 30. Batching interviews into one scheduled event, instead of weeks of calendar back-and-forth, is the main lever you have 11.

Applications per hire tripled, and nobody added recruiters

Recruiters now handle roughly 291 applications per hire, up from about 100 in early 2021 29, with workloads climbing 93% year over year 31. Much of that inbound is duplicated or auto-generated, so the extra volume buys screening hours rather than candidates.

Every hour spent triaging a bigger pile is an hour not spent selling the role or closing a finalist, which is the work that brings cost per hire down. In our topline report with SHRM Labs, 25.2% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners said they have never measured how much recruiter time goes to screening versus strategic work.

Cost Per Hire by Role: $1,500 Entry to $250,000 Retained

Both halves, invoiced and not, scale with the role. Cost per hire moves in bands by seniority, so a blended average tells you almost nothing about the job you are about to open. Entry-level and retained executive search sit two orders of magnitude apart.

What widens the gap between bands

Two things push a role up its band, and only one of them shows on an invoice.

  • Specialization: when talent is scarce, recruiters spend more hours sourcing per hire and teams lean harder on paid channels and agencies 34.
  • Interview intensity: senior and specialized roles need more rounds and longer sourcing, which raises recruiter and manager time per hire 34.

None of this covers a hire that fails. Replacement is a separate metric, and folding it in is how the two figures stop being comparable.

Hiring Volume, Not Company Size, Sets What You Pay per Hire

Seniority is one axis. Volume is the other. Much of your recruiting spend is fixed, and the number of hires it gets divided across is not. Same budget, very different cost per hire. That is the whole mechanic.

A 200-person company making 40 hires on $120,000 of recruiting spend lands at $3,000 each 27. A smaller company spends less overall and still pays more per hire. It has no dedicated recruiting team, so it leans on agencies and paid postings. Under 50 employees, that often runs past $7,500 a hire 35. An enterprise spreads its software licenses, recruiter salaries and employer branding over hundreds of hires 7.

A low hiring budget is not evidence of efficient hiring. It is usually evidence of a small denominator.

The breakeven sits around 15 hires a year

In-house or agency is a volume question. An internal recruiting function costs $146,000 to $200,000 a year whether it fills 30 roles or three 36. Agency spend is purely variable, so below roughly 15-20 hires a year the agency is cheaper, and above it the fixed team wins 36.

Treat that as a midpoint, not a threshold. Published estimates run from six or seven hires up to 30 or 40 37. They disagree mostly because they price different things: one recruiter with a lean tool stack, or a full team with sourcing and admin support 38.

Two things move your own crossover point. Whether hiring demand is steady enough to keep a recruiter busy through a slow quarter, and whether the roles are specialized enough that an internal generalist cannot reach the candidates at all.

The one model where more hires means a lower cost per hire

Flat-rate hiring events push that mechanic furthest, because the fee is fixed per event and the hire count is not 11. Across 582 healthcare hiring events in 2025, employers averaged eight hires each, which works out to under $200 a hire at the top pricing tier before staff time 11. It only holds for multi-role, hourly and clinical hiring, where interview volume is the bottleneck.

The Vacancy Cost SHRM's Formula Leaves Out: $500 a Day

An empty seat costs money every day it stays empty, and the SHRM/ANSI formula counts none of it 10. For an average professional role, one estimate puts that loss at about $500 a day in output 10. Set that against SHRM's 2026 median time to fill of 39 days for non-executive roles, and the open seat costs roughly $19,500 before a single recruiting invoice arrives 9.

Sales roles lose more, $7,000 to $10,000 a month in deals that never close 10. Executive searches run longer, at a median of 45 days 8.

Pressure to fill fast pushes teams toward agencies and sponsored listings, the most expensive channels available 12. So a slow process raises both halves of the number at once.

Four ways the figure stops being defensible

  • Match the period on both sides. Annual spend over a quarterly hire count is not a cost per hire. Count hires whole, never as fractions of a full-time employee 6.
  • Segment before you compare. Average a bulk hiring push with one specialized search and you misstate both 34.
  • Include every search, not just the wins. All spend in a period goes over all hires in that period, failed searches included.
  • Read cost next to quality of hire. Only 20% of organizations track both, and a cheap hire who leaves at month six was never cheap 4.

The first three protect the arithmetic. The fourth protects the decision, because cost falls fastest when the bar drops.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average cost per hire in the US?

The current SHRM benchmark is $5,475 for a non-executive hire, an average from the 2025 Benchmarking Report 1. The U.S. median sits near $1,633, because high-volume entry-level roles drag it down 32.

How do you lower cost per hire without dropping hiring quality?

Cut interview hours before you cut spend. Screen candidates more sharply early on, batch your interview panels, and pay for referrals. Referred hires cost 40-60% less than agency placements and stay longer 27.

Does cost per hire include onboarding costs?

No. The count stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding falls outside it 11. Track training and ramp time separately, or the two figures stop being comparable across periods.

What is the difference between cost per hire and time to fill?

Cost per hire is money spent per filled seat. Time to fill is the days from open role to accepted offer. A slow fill raises cost, because an empty professional seat loses about $500 a day 10.

How much does it cost to replace an employee who leaves?

Replacement is a separate metric, and folding it into cost per hire breaks the comparison. Common estimates run from six to nine months of the leaver's salary, and climb sharply for senior roles.
