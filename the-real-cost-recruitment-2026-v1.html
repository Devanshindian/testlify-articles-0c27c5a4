The Real Cost Per Hire in 2026: The 2016 Figure Everyone Quotes

3,242 words · 11 sections · 9 sources

Your budget probably says a cost per hire of $4,129. That number came from a survey run in spring 2016. The Society for Human Resource Management (SHRM) now puts a nonexecutive hire near $5,475 on its 2025 average series and an executive hire at a $15,000 median on its 2026 series 1. Quote the old $8,800 executive median in 2026 and you are off by about 70% of it.

Even the new figures only count what someone invoiced you. That is an estimated 30-40% of what a hire really costs. Before you put a number in front of a CFO, know which edition it came from, how it was worked out, and what it leaves out.

The $4,129 Figure Was Collected in Spring 2016

The $4,700 figure that replaced $4,129 comes from a cycle SHRM now calls superseded. People still quote both in 2026 as if they were current. Neither one is.

Start with the older one. SHRM published $4,129 in its Human Capital Benchmarking Report in August 2016, using answers from 2,048 members about fiscal year 2015. So the number sitting in hiring budgets today describes what hiring looked like a decade ago.

The $4,700 figure is younger, but it is not current either. SHRM published it on April 11, 2022, in an article titled "The Real Costs of Recruitment" 2. The article calls it an average and then tells you nothing else: no report title, no edition year, no field dates, no sample size 2.

The cycle behind it used answers collected from April through November 2021 from more than 2,400 members. In October 2025 SHRM said its new study updates those 2022 benchmarking studies.

The mistake is quoting any cost of hire number without its edition, its averaging method, and its sample. SHRM does not make respondents answer every question, so the sample behind one figure shifts inside a single edition. Ask which release, which method, and how many companies answered that exact question. A number that cannot survive those three questions cannot hold up a hiring budget.

Why SHRM's Average Is $5,475 and Its Median $15,000

Which SHRM number is right depends on which series you picked up. The 2025 benchmarking release reports averages: $5,475 for a nonexecutive hire and $35,879 for an executive one, so an executive costs nearly seven times more. The 2026 recruiting brief reports medians instead, which is why its executive figure reads $15,000 rather than $35,879 1. SHRM uses medians because a few very high or very low answers cannot drag them around, which makes years easier to compare.

The samples changed too. The 2025 survey ran online from January 9 to March 3, 2025, leaned toward vice presidents and above, and drew 2,371 respondents. The 2026 report used 4,657 members, leaned toward director level and above, and started collecting on November 24, 2025 1. So keep the rule tight: compare averages to averages, medians to medians, and only inside the same published series.

How to Calculate Cost Per Hire, and Where It Stops

Cost per hire is everything you spend to find, attract, and assess one new employee. SHRM treats one formula as standard: CPH = (Total Internal Recruiting Costs + Total External Recruiting Costs) ÷ Total Number of Hires. You divide what you spent in a period by the hires you made in that same period. Two buckets feed it, which is why two honest finance teams can run the same formula and land on different numbers.

Internal costs are the ones your own company absorbs:

  • Recruiter pay and overhead: salaries, benefits, taxes, and overhead for your in-house talent acquisition (TA) team.
  • Hiring manager and interviewer time: panel hours priced at a loaded hourly rate, debriefs included. Teams understate this line more than any other.
  • HR administrative time: coordinator scheduling and system updates.
  • Recruiting technology: the Applicant Tracking System (ATS), sourcing tools, and assessment platforms.
  • Referral bonuses and recruiting events: internal referral payouts, job fairs, and campus recruiting.

External costs are the invoices from outside vendors:

  • Job board postings and advertising: per-posting and subscription fees, plus employer brand campaigns.
  • Agency and retained search fees: typically 20-30% of first-year salary for contingency work, and 30-35% for retained executive search.
  • Background checks and assessments: roughly $20-$100 per screening check and $30-$200 per candidate assessed.
  • Sign-on bonuses and relocation: count them when you need them to close the hire.

Now the boundary. Under the SHRM standard, counting stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding, vacancy, and ramp all sit outside it. Everything past that line is still a hiring cost. It just is not in the benchmark.

Hard Costs Are Only 30-40% of What a Hire Costs

The SHRM formula sets the benchmark, not the whole bill. Hard costs run 30-40% of what you spend on hiring, and time-based soft costs are the other 60-70%. That gap is why a benchmark and a real hiring budget almost never match.

Hard costs land on invoices and card statements: job ads, agency and search-firm fees, screening and assessment tools, recruiting software, relocation, and employer payroll taxes. Finance can track them because every line has a price.

The soft side is where the money hides: recruiter and hiring manager hours, interview time from the rest of your team, the work nobody does while a seat sits empty, ramp-up, training, knowledge transfer, and the damage a bad hire does to a team. Edie Goldberg, SHRM Foundation chair-elect and co-author of The Inside Gig, put the split at "30 percent to 40 percent are hard costs, and the other 60 percent are soft costs" 2.

That is one expert's estimate from a 2022 interview, not a number measured from SHRM survey data 2. Use it to plan, not to benchmark.

Follow that ratio out and the total stops looking anything like the headline.

Most calculators only count what finance already paid for. They miss the hours your managers lose to interviews, the tools you bought and barely use, the candidates who never show up, and the work nobody does while the seat sits empty. Recruiter hours at least get talked about. Manager and panel hours usually go uncounted.

So carry two lines in your budget, not one. The benchmark covers the invoiced 30-40%. Everything else in this article sits in the other 60-70%.

Agency Fees: 20-35% of Salary, 40-50% for C-Suite

Agency fees are the biggest outside cost when you fill professional or senior roles. Firms charge a share of first-year salary, so you pay more for senior people. Nothing about that line is fixed.

Three fee structures cover almost every deal. Contingency search is the usual one for mid-level roles: 20-30% of first-year salary, paid only when a candidate accepts 3. You can run several contingency firms at once and pay only the one that places your hire. The catch is that each firm gives your search less time and reaches fewer passive candidates.

Retained search runs about 30-35% of salary, sometimes plus expenses, paid in installments as each stage finishes. You are buying exclusivity and a firm that stays on your search. Hybrid deals, called container or retingency arrangements, take 10-25% of the total up front and the rest on placement 4.

Treat 20-35% as an opening number, not a price list. Seniority and how many candidates exist move it. Recruiters on both sides of the table will tell you the quoted rate often comes down.

The percentage climbs with the role, and so does the salary it applies to. Most companies skip agencies for junior roles. From manager to VP, permanent placement fees usually run 20-30% of base salary. C-level searches are the priciest in the market, hitting 40-50% of first-year income at the top end 4.

Watch the dollars, not the percentage. A $120,000 engineering hire costs $24,000 to $36,000 in agency fees. For C-suite and board searches, some firms set a hard floor no matter the pay, starting at $75,000. Executive deals also add admin fees of 10-15%, plus travel, background checks, and job ads, which can add another 5-15% to the total 4.

Technical Hires Cost 23.3 Hours of Interview Time

Interview time is the biggest soft cost still inside the benchmark. A technical hire takes 23.3 hours of interview time. A business hire takes 12.2. Those 11 extra hours come from engineers and managers who do not work in recruiting, and no invoice ever shows them.

The load keeps growing. Technical roles now average 17.6 interviews per hire, up 52% from roughly 11 in 2021 5. Business roles average 11.7, up 36% 5.

Split it by function and Data leads at 24.9 hours per hire. Engineering is right behind at 24.7, and Customer Support sits at the bottom with 8.9 6. That is a more than threefold spread 6.

The spread matters as much as the average. Budget on the mean and you will underprice a specialized engineering search. One founder priced a senior engineering loop at roughly $15,000 of engineering time against about $4,000 of recruiting time 7. The gap comes from counting what it costs to interrupt an engineer 7.

Batch the interviews into one scheduled event. In high-volume hiring that is the biggest lever you have. Spread the same interviews across weeks of calendar back-and-forth and you buy nothing but delay.

The Vacancy and Ramp Costs SHRM Leaves Out

The SHRM/ANSI standard stops at the accepted offer, so two of your biggest hiring costs sit outside it. One is the empty seat. The other is the months before your new hire is fully productive. Neither shows up on an invoice, and both are money.

Start with the vacancy. Deloitte's recruitment efficiency research puts an unfilled average professional role at about $500 per day in lost productivity. Specialized or revenue-generating roles cost more.

The median time to fill is 63.5 days in 2026, down from 67.7 days in 2025. At $500 a day across 44 working days, one standard vacancy costs $22,000 before a recruiter spends a cent.

Revenue roles run higher. A senior sales seat at $2,500 a day over 45 days reaches $112,500.

Ramp time is harder to pin down, so be honest rather than precise. Published onboarding costs swing by three to five times depending on what each source counts. One benchmark puts average U.S. onboarding at $1,500 to $5,000 per employee, covering training, mentoring, and the first round of HR processing.

The drag underneath lasts longer than the invoice suggests. Research cited by the Association for Talent Development finds a professional hire reaches full productivity three to nine months after starting. You pay full salary the whole time for partial output. Leave that out and your cost to hire is badly understated.

Stack all three and the benchmark looks small. Take the $5,475 SHRM average, add $22,000 of vacancy, then add onboarding at the top of its published band. The loaded cost lands near six times the headline. Whatever number you defend to a CFO should say which of those three layers it counts.

In-House vs Agency: The 15-20 Hires a Year Breakeven

Volume moves cost per hire as much as role type does. Some of your recruiting spend is fixed, so the more hires you make, the thinner it spreads. A 40-person company fills eight roles in a quarter for $6,000 in external costs and $9,000 in internal costs, and lands at $1,875 per hire. A bigger company spending $100,000 a year on HR salaries and hiring tools plus $75,000 on external recruiting services, across 50 hires, comes to $3,500.

The smaller number does not mean the smaller team recruits better. It is simply not filling executive or specialized technical roles at the rate a large enterprise does. Small companies usually pay more per hire while spending less in total, because they have no scale, no dedicated recruiting team, and lean harder on agencies and job board postings.

Make five to 10 hires a year instead of 500 and every hiring decision hits the budget hard. An enterprise spreads ATS licenses, recruiter salaries, and employer branding across hundreds or thousands of hires.

One channel flips the math. A flat-rate hiring event costs the same whether it produces four hires or 40, so cost per hire drops as volume climbs. It only works for multi-role, hourly, and clinical hiring, where interview volume is the bottleneck.

Only 20% Pair Cost Per Hire With Quality of Hire

Cost per hire only holds up when you report it next to quality of hire. Only 20% of organizations track the two together. If your cost falls while ninety-day retention falls with it, you did not save money, you delayed it. Report both, or the attrition shows up in next quarter's data anyway.

Four rules keep the math honest. Match the period on both sides, because annual costs over a monthly hire count gives you nonsense. Count whole hires, not fractions of full-time equivalents.

Split by role level, department, and hiring type before you compare anything. A bulk hiring push averaged against one specialized search misstates both. Skip cross-company benchmarking altogether, since industry, role mix, geography, and methodology all move the figure.

That last rule is the one people break most. Compare yourself to your own past periods first. Ask any team that has tried it: the outside number was never the argument that landed.

Frequently asked questions

How do I reduce cost per hire?

Batch interviews into one scheduled event, the biggest lever in high-volume hiring. Then match your sourcing model to your volume: under 15-20 hires a year, agencies cost less than an in-house team. Report either move next to quality of hire.

What is the difference between cost per hire and time to fill?

Cost per hire measures money spent per hire. Time to fill measures days from open role to accepted offer, a median of 63.5 days in 2026. Every vacant day costs you about $500 in lost productivity.

Should internal promotions count in cost per hire?

The SHRM formula divides recruiting costs by total hires in the period. Whether internal moves count is your call, but pick one rule and hold it. Report internal fills separately, since they cost far less.

What is recruitment cost ratio?

Recruitment cost ratio is your total recruiting spend divided by the hire's first-year compensation, written as a percentage. It moves with salary, so it compares an executive search to an entry-level fill more fairly than a flat dollar figure.

How often should you recalculate cost per hire?

Quarterly, on a rolling 12 months, so one expensive search does not skew a short window. Recalculate sooner if your role mix or hiring volume changes, because both move the figure more than recruiting efficiency does.
