# Curriculum Vitae Statistics Everyone Cites Are Out of Date

You are building the slide. You need one number for how long a recruiter spends on a CV. The first three results hand you three different figures. Not one carries a year or a sample size. So you pick the one that reads best and hope nobody in the room asks where it came from.

Most curriculum vitae statistics in circulation travel without the four things that make them usable. Those four are the publisher, the year, the sample, and the question that was actually put to people. This page takes the figures you are most likely to inherit and attaches those four labels, or says plainly when one does not exist.

## TL;DR

- The six-second CV scan was retired by its own publisher in 2018, and the 7.4-second replacement never disclosed how many recruiters it watched.
- The lying rates only look like a disagreement because the surveys asked different questions: 44% covers one hiring process, 64.2% covers an entire career.
- Among candidates already using AI in their job search, the majority admit using it to exaggerate their skills, which is the figure most roundups leave out.
- No applicant tracking system is auto-rejecting three quarters of CVs. Application volume does the culling, and it is recruiters who decide where to stop reading.

## The Six-Second Resume Scan Was Retired in 2018

"Recruiters spend six seconds on your CV" is the most repeated statistic in hiring. The company that produced it stopped standing behind the number years ago. It came from a 2012 eye-tracking study by TheLadders, which timed recruiters making a first fit-or-no-fit judgment [1]. The same publisher ran the study again in 2018. That second run put the average at 7.4 seconds [1]. The correction never caught up. A round six makes a better headline than a decimal, so the retired version is still the one you meet first.

There was never a single number to retire. In 2023, Jan [Tegze](https://www.distinctrecruitment.com/uk/resources/blog/the-6-second-cv-recruitments-biggest-myth/) showed 114 recruiters three CVs of different lengths. Tegze timed every review. The averages ran from 17 seconds on the shortest to 46 seconds on the longest [2]. The document set the clock, not some fixed recruiter habit. If you are coaching candidates on a two-page CV using a six-second benchmark, you are quoting an experiment nobody ran on that document.

### What neither Ladders study ever disclosed

Both Ladders figures name a publisher and a year, which already puts them ahead of most CV statistics in circulation. They fall down on the sample. The 2012 study timed 30 recruiters. The 2018 update never states how many people took part at all [2]. Thirty is a small room of people, and an unstated number is not a sample, it is an assertion.

None of that makes 7.4 seconds wrong. It makes it uncheckable, which is the more awkward problem for whoever puts it in a board deck. When a director asks how many recruiters were watched, or what kind of roles they were screening for, you have no answer to give. There is no paper to send afterwards.

So use the figure the way you would use any average built from decisions that are not alike. A graduate applying for a VP role is dismissed almost immediately. A well-matched senior candidate gets a real read. One mean covers both, which is fine as background and useless as a rule. If you are about to write "no CV longer than one page" into a hiring policy on the strength of it, you are turning an average with no published spread into a standard your recruiters have to defend.

## Who Admits to Lying on a CV, and When They Were Asked

The headline lying rates look like a disagreement. Mostly they are a difference in the question. [ResumeBuilder](https://www.resumebuilder.com/resume-examples/1-4-americans-have-lied-on-their-resume/) asked candidates in January 2025 whether they had lied anywhere in the hiring process, and 44% said yes. [StandOut CV](https://standout-cv.com/usa/stats-usa/study-fake-job-references-resume-lies) asked more than 2,100 Americans whether they had ever [lied on a CV](https://testlify.com/candidate-dishonesty/) across a whole career. That survey returned 64.2%. Both numbers are honest. They are not the same measurement. Presenting the two together as a range from 44% to 64% invents a spread that neither survey found.

Who you ask moves the figure as much as what you ask. Admitted lying peaks at 80.4% among 18-to-25-year-olds in the StandOut CV data. Among older respondents it falls to 46.9%. A sample that leans young reports a higher rate for reasons that have nothing to do with the year. If you are hiring mid-career professionals, quoting 64.2% at your leadership team overstates what is sitting in your own pipeline.

### The 32% is from 2021, and the 55% "from 2022" was never a survey

The older rates still in circulation carry dates too. Those dates change what they mean. The most quoted of them is 32%. It comes from a ResumeBuilder survey fielded in July 2021, while pandemic-era hiring was rebounding and application volume was climbing. It remains a usable number, as a 2021 reading of a recovering labour market. Set it beside a 2025 figure with the year stripped off, and a four-year rise starts to look like two sources contradicting each other.

The 55% has a weaker claim to existence. StandOut CV's own report frames its result as up from 55% in 2022. No independent 2022 survey with its own sample or method stands behind that baseline. The only parent that number has is a sentence in a later report by the same publisher. Someone in the room will eventually ask you for the survey, and there is nothing to send them.

The working rule is short. Before a lying rate goes into anything with your name on it, find the fieldwork month, the country and the question's exact wording. If one of the three is still missing after ten minutes of searching, leave the figure out.

## How Many CVs Are Now Written With AI

In 2025, 52% of candidates told Employ's Job Seeker Nation survey that they use AI to write or polish their CVs. Roughly half of every pile now passes through a language model on its way to you. That alone should retire a habit most hiring teams still have. They read clean, confident prose as evidence of a careful applicant. It is now evidence of a free tool.

The figure carries a caveat of exactly the kind this article exists to surface. Within Employ's own series it is a fall, because the 2024 edition of the same report put AI use at 58%. Adoption numbers rarely drop. Either candidates got quieter about admitting it, or the sample shifted. Quote it as Employ, 2025, self-reported, and the movement is interesting. Quote it bare and it turns into a trend line pointing the wrong way.

The number that changes your process is the next one along. Among job seekers already using AI in their search, 83% admit using it to exaggerate or lie about their skills. Read that base carefully, because it is where most write-ups go wrong. The figure describes people who already reached for the tool. It does not describe every candidate in your funnel. A slide claiming that 83% of all applicants lie with AI will not survive its first fact check.

What it does tell you is that polish and fabrication now arrive in the same document. Tidying up grammar is not the problem, and most employers never objected to it. The problem is the invented achievement sitting three bullets below, written in the same tone. That is a reason to ask better questions about the claims on the page, not to start scoring the prose.

## Employers Reject AI CVs They Cannot Reliably Spot

Hiring managers do reject applications they believe a machine wrote, and they are confident they can identify them. In a 2025 survey, 74% of hiring managers said they can spot [AI-generated content](https://testlify.com/detect-fake-resumes-in-hiring/). That confidence is not academic. It decides which candidates get a call. The decision usually lands in the first minute of a review, and usually nobody writes down the reason.

### 74% say they can spot AI writing; in 2023, 82% could not

Two years earlier, ResumeBuilder put the question to 1,000 hiring managers and found that 82% could not reliably identify a ChatGPT-written cover letter. Stack the two figures on one slide and you cannot tell what happened in between. Detection may have improved. Or one survey asked people to rate themselves and the other tested them. Those are different exercises, and they produce predictably different answers.

The false-positive evidence should worry anyone acting on either number. Standalone AI-detection tools flag 30 to 50% of human-written resumes as AI-generated. Run 400 applications through one of those tools and you have accused roughly 150 people who wrote every word themselves. Most of them never learn it happened. Non-native English speakers and anyone who writes in a plain, formal register absorb the bulk of that error.

So treat the writing as a poor signal and go after the claims instead. What experienced recruiters catch is not robotic phrasing but a quantified achievement that falls apart under one follow-up question. Ask the candidate to walk you through the project behind the number. The answer arrives in about two minutes, with no detector involved and no honest applicant wrongly thrown out.

## The 75% ATS Rejection Rate Was a 2012 Sales Pitch

The claim that software automatically rejects 75% of resumes traces back to Preptel. Preptel was a resume-optimization startup that went out of business in 2013. No published methodology, sample size or primary document sits behind the number. Note who was selling what. A company whose product was resume optimization produced the statistic that made resume optimization necessary, and the industry has been quoting it back to itself ever since.

What the software actually does is duller. Only 8% of recruiters set any auto-rejection logic in their [applicant tracking system](https://testlify.com/hr-glossary/applicant-tracking-system-ats/). The other 92% confirm their system rejects nothing at all on formatting or content. The system parses, stores and ranks. It almost never decides. The black hole candidates describe is mostly a queue nobody reached the bottom of, which is a different problem with a different fix.

The pressure creating that queue is arithmetic. Applications on Workday's platforms grew 31% year on year in the first half of 2024, while the number of openings grew 7%. Demand rose more than four times faster than the roles absorbing it. A recruiter works down a ranked list until the role closes, and everything below the line is never read rather than rejected. If you want to lose fewer good candidates, look at how your system ranks and how deep your team gets into the list. Do not look at whether someone used the wrong font.

## The Year, Sample and Method Behind Each CV Statistic

People quote these figures without the labels that make them mean anything. That is where most of the damage comes from. The table below attaches publisher, year, sample and method to the four figures you are most likely to be handed, so any of them can go into an appendix. Each one will survive a reader who checks.

| Figure | Publisher and year | Sample and method | What it actually measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.4-second CV scan | Ladders, 2018  | Eye-tracking; sample never disclosed | How long a first screen lasted, observed rather than self-reported |
| 64.2% lied on a CV | StandOut CV, 2024 survey [3] | 2,100+ Americans, self-report | Ever lied on a CV, across an entire career |
| 44% lied during hiring | ResumeBuilder, January 2025 [4] | 2,000 Americans who had applied for jobs, self-report | Lying anywhere in one hiring process, with 24% on the CV itself |
| 52% use AI on CVs | Employ, Job Seeker Nation 2025  | More than 1,500 job seekers, self-report | Writing or reviewing a CV with AI, down from 58% in 2024 |

Read down the last two columns and the supposed contradictions disappear. An observed timing and a self-reported one are not rivals. A lifetime question and a single-process question were always going to return different numbers. Carry the labels with the figure and you can defend it in the meeting where it matters.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can ATS detect ChatGPT resume?**

No. Applicant tracking systems parse and rank a CV, they detect nothing. Even purpose-built detectors flag 30 to 50% of human-written resumes as AI-generated, and a light edit after generating usually gets past them.

**What are red flags on a CV?**

Unexplained gaps, titles that outrun the dates, and any achievement the candidate cannot walk you through. Uniform action-verb bullets and suspiciously round percentages suggest a model helped write it, which is not the same as a lie.

**How do you verify the skills a CV claims?**

Pick one project from the page. Ask the candidate to walk you through it in detail: what they built, what broke, and what they would do differently. Invented work collapses quickly. A structured skills test gives you the same evidence at volume.

**Is a CV the same thing as a resume?**

No. A resume is a short, tailored summary of one or two pages, standard in the US. A CV is a fuller chronological record of a career. It is standard in the UK, Europe and academia.

**How long should a CV be?**

Two pages for experienced hires, one for early-career candidates. Length changes the read. A 2023 test of 114 recruiters found average review time ran from 17 seconds to 46 by CV length [2].
