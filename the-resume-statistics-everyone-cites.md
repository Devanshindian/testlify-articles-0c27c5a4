# The Curriculum Vitae Statistics Everyone Cites Are Outdated

You put curriculum vitae statistics in a deck, someone asks where the number came from, and the honest answer is often a blog post that said “studies show.” Six of the most-cited ATS (Applicant Tracking System) rejection numbers trace back to no study at all, and the famous six-second resume scan is a decade old, quietly raised by the firm behind it years ago. One number is missing from most roundups: 83% of job seekers who use AI admit they invent or pad resume skills. Every stat here carries its year.

## Quick answer

Most resume numbers in hiring decks are old, undated, or blended from surveys that asked different things in different years. The famous six-second scan comes from a study its own source later raised to 7.4 seconds, the most-quoted lying rate comes from one survey run in a very tight job market, and several of the best-known ATS rejection numbers trace to no study at all. The biggest gap is what roundups skip: 83% of job seekers who use AI say they used it to invent or pad skills, not to draft faster. Any number worth citing needs its year and its source attached.

## Key Takeaways

- The six-second scan is now 7.4 seconds, per the firm that ran both studies.
- Screen time tracks with length: 17 seconds for a short CV, 46 for a long one.
- The one-in-three lying rate is one July 2021 survey, taken in a hot job market [1].
- 52% of job seekers now write with AI. Of those, 83% admit they invent or pad skills.
- 92% of tracking systems auto-reject nobody. Volume does the cutting.
- Six of the most-quoted rejection figures name no sample, year or method.

## TheLadders Already Revised Its Own 6-Second Stat

The six-second resume scan comes from a 2012 eye-tracking study [TheLadders](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/) ran with its own recruiters. It found an average of six seconds before a fit or no-fit call. TheLadders did not let that number stand.

In 2018 it ran a two-part follow-up. One part timed recruiters who did not know they were being timed. The other tracked their eyes across the page [2]. The new average came out at 7.4 seconds, about a second more over six years.

That fix has sat inside TheLadders' own research for years, while people citing the figure still stop at 2012. One review found it in articles as late as 2023. The gap grew its own genre: “beat the six-second CV” quizzes, mostly run by sites selling resume templates. Many pages recycling the number link to no study at all, and just write “studies have shown”.

## What Critics Say the Studies Got Wrong

Critics have picked apart what the study does not say. The 2012 version named its sample: 30 recruiters. The 2018 update never says how many took part, how they were picked, or what gear was used.

The job posts and resumes were never published, so nobody knows how senior the roles were. Nothing was peer-reviewed, so no outside team could run it again. Critics also note that hiring in the UK works in ways the study never tested. One critique argues there is “a strong chance this study was never even carried out”.

The number is an average, not a rule. A new grad who applies for a top job may be dropped in a fraction of a second, while a close match gets a longer look. Cite it as TheLadders, 2018, 7.4 seconds. A scan that short decides where the eye goes, not whether a line is true.

## How Long Recruiters Actually Spend on a Resume

TheLadders' 7.4 seconds was never the only count, just the loudest. A 2023 study found screen time tracks with how long the CV is. Jan Tegze, of LinkedIn's Top Voices group, had 114 recruiters read three CVs of different lengths. The short one drew 17 seconds, the mid-length one 27, the long one 46.

One reviewer spent 136.2 seconds on the longest CV, 22.7 times the six-second figure. Heat maps from the same study show recruiters working the first page and skimming the rest. In Southeast Asia, recruiters reviewed CVs through InterviewPal's review tool. The first scan there averaged 11.2 seconds, well above the 6-8 second mark people quote.

Those recruiters had an AI tool showing the job post and key skills beside the CV, so it was not a bare human scan. A full review took 1 minute 34 seconds at the median, most of it spent checking job titles and hard numbers. Cursor tracking shows where the eye lands, and layout, the thing most resume advice frets about, comes last:

| Section | Share of gaze time |
|---|---|
| Work history | 38%  |
| Summary and headline | 24%  |
| Skills | 18%  |
| Schooling | 11%  |
| Layout and design | 9%  |

## Why Screening Times Disagree

Layout changes the clock and the outcome. The InterviewPal data tied slower, kinder reads to number-backed wins, matching keywords and simple one-column formatting. Dense blocks and mixed formatting made up 43% of the resumes recruiters turned down at screening.

Which pass a CV is getting matters too. One that already cleared a first screen gets a slower, closer read by design. Roles covered by rules take longer to check than roles wanting general work history.

## The 2021 Survey Behind the 32% Lying Stat

The most-cited figure for [lying on a resume](https://testlify.com/candidate-dishonesty/), about one in three, comes from one [ResumeBuilder](https://www.resumebuilder.com/1-in-3-americans-admit-to-lying-on-resume/).com survey of 1,250 U.S. adults: 32% said they had lied [1]. It ran in July 2021, a month when the U.S. added 934,000 jobs [1], so it captures one moment in a very tight job market, not a constant.

People stretch a real skill more often than they invent one:

1. Years of work, named by 46% of those who admitted lying.
2. Schooling, named by 44%.
3. Puffed-up skills, named by 40%.

The reason is plain: 72% lied to improve their odds of being hired. Another 41% were covering up a firing or a bad exit [1].

The rate was far from even. Among people earning $100,000 to $149,000, 49% admitted lying, against 25% of those under $99,999. IT roles led at 55%, then finance at 45%.

## Who Lies on a Resume, and How We'd Know

Two lying figures disagree mostly because they do not ask the same thing. One asks if you ever lied across a whole career, another about a single hiring round, a third only about the past twelve months. Fraud files count proven cases instead of self-reports.

| Figure | Source | What it asked |
|---|---|---|
| 64.2% | StandOut-CV, 2025  | Ever lied, whole career |
| 44% | ResumeBuilder, 2025  | Lied in one hiring round |
| 32% | ResumeBuilder, July 2021 [1] | Ever lied, hot job market |
| ~10% | UK adults [3] | Lied in the past twelve months |
| 55% | No study at all  | Nothing; see below |

Rates swing by tens of points across sources, and the pages repeating them rarely say which of these moved. That 55% is not a data point: it shows up inside a 2025 StandOut-CV report as “up from 55% in 2022”.

[Cifas](https://www.cifas.org.uk/newsroom/false-qualifications-cv-fraud), the UK fraud file, counts proven cases. It marked 33% of what it handled in 2023 as false job forms, down from 44% in 2022. That is not fewer liars. Job fraud was simply a smaller slice of the year's cases.

## AI-Written Resumes Went From Rare to Routine

Half of all job seekers now write their resume with AI. Employ's 2025 Job Seeker Nation Report puts it at 52%. Three years earlier the share was too small to show up.

1. **2022:** ChatGPT-written resumes were a tiny share; the tool had just launched.
2. **2023:** Interest ran ahead of use. Google searches for “AI resumes” rose 1,592% across the year.
3. **Late 2024:** ResumeLab and Jobscan put AI-written resumes at 30-40% of what some tech firms received.
4. **2025:** Employ's survey puts it at 52% of job seekers.
5. **2027, projected:** AI-written resumes pass human-written ones among job seekers under 30.

What the AI is doing to the resume is the harder question.

## Can Hiring Managers Actually Spot an AI-Written Resume?

Hiring managers cannot agree with themselves here, let alone with each other. In one 2024 survey, 80% throw out resumes they think AI wrote, yet only 74% say they can spot one for sure [11]. Three more 2025 surveys, from Resume.io, Resume Now and Resume Genius, each asked a different question and got a different number [13].

Blend those into one snapshot and you hide the uncertainty: nobody knows whether managers got better, or whether the surveys measured different things. When managers do act, the triggers are narrow:

- **No personal detail:** 62% in the Resume Now survey drop an AI-written resume once it reads as generic [13].
- **Suspicion alone:** in the Resume.io survey, managers bin a resume the moment they suspect AI [13].
- **Tone, not errors:** the tell they name most is writing too smooth to be the person's own [33].

Tools built to catch this do no better. Outside testing puts their wrong-flag rate at 30-50%, so up to half the resumes they flag were written by a person [33]. Light edits to an AI draft often slip past them [33].

## How Often AI Resume Help Turns Into Lying

Among job seekers who use AI, 83% admit they used it to pad or invent skills on a resume, cover letter, job form or [skills test](https://testlify.com/ai-pre-hire-assessments/). That is the number most roundups skip. A 2023/2024 survey found 73.4% would consider using AI to help them lie in the year ahead.

Someone who used AI to tighten a summary is not the same risk as someone who used it to invent a degree. On the page, the two look alike.

Three tells keep showing up, all rooted in how the models were trained:

- **Same bullet shape:** every line opens with an action verb, because that is what the models learned to write.
- **Round wins:** “Cut costs by 30%” turns up more often than odd numbers would, because the training data is full of round figures.
- **Claims that cannot be true:** a model cannot tell that “led a team of 40” makes no sense for an intern.

None of these proves anything alone. When one resume stacks all three, ask a direct question before the file moves on.

## The ATS Doesn't Auto-Reject Resumes, Volume Does

Almost every big US employer runs one. 97.8% of Fortune 500 firms use an [Applicant Tracking System](https://testlify.com/hr-glossary/applicant-tracking-system-ats/), or ATS, the software that logs and sorts every resume before a recruiter opens it. The AI inside now reads the substance too, work history, years on the job, skill set, then grades the person into a tier. Systems with live AI ranking handle 79.3% of Fortune 500 hiring.

That sounds like a machine doing the rejecting. Mostly it is not. An Enhancv survey found 92% of these systems do not auto-reject on format or content. Jobscan, one of the biggest vendors here, says the software stores resumes for keyword search rather than binning them.

What keeps a resume from a human is volume. [Workday](https://newsroom.workday.com/2024-09-10-Workday-Global-Workforce-Report-Job-Market-Tightens-as-AI-Reshapes-Hiring-Processes)'s platform took 173 million job forms in the first half of 2024, up 31% on the year before [4]. Openings on those platforms grew only 7%, to 19 million [4]. Resumes arrive about four times faster than roles open.

Recruiters work down a ranked list until the role is filled, and it often closes long before the list runs out. No code rejected those resumes; nobody ever saw them. A deck that blames the code blames the wrong step.

## The ATS Rejection Numbers You've Probably Cited Aren't Real

None of the ATS rejection figures that circulate trace to a stated method, sample size or dataset. They sit on job-advice sites with no survey year and no named source, and they get repeated because they sound right. Six of the most-cited ones fail that test.

| Claim | Cited source | What's missing |
|---|---|---|
| ATS drops 20-60% of resumes before a human sees them | Cornell  | No stated method or sample size  |
| ATS drops up to 75% of resumes | Cornell  | No source you can trace  |
| 98% of resumes sent to an online job post get rejected | CareerLauncher  | No date, no method  |
| 70% of resumes are rejected at the first screen | LinkedIn  | Undated; the screen “could either be by a software system or recruiter”  |
| Fewer than 1 in 10 resumes reach the hiring manager | none named  | No date, sample or method  |
| An “ideal” resume scored a 43% match across several ATS tools | Bersin & Associates  | No year for the test  |

Each runs in trade articles as settled fact. None is one. A number with no year, no sample and no stated method is not proof, just a claim borrowed from a blog that borrowed it elsewhere.

## How Often Background Checks Catch Resume Lies

Checks catch what a resume cannot show on its own. ResumeBuilder found nearly half the people hired on a lie lost the offer once it came out [1]. ResumeLab found that of those caught, 65% were either not hired or fired. Getting hired despite a lie is common; keeping the job once it surfaces is not.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are red flags on a CV?**

Same-shaped action-verb bullets, tidy round figures, and wins that do not match the level of the role often point to AI text. Also watch for mixed job titles and dense, badly set text.

**Can ATS detect ChatGPT resume?**

Most ATS tools score a resume for job fit and keyword match, not for who wrote it. Standalone AI detectors flag the wrong text 30-50% of the time, and light edits slip past them [33].

**Should you disclose that AI helped write your resume?**

Some managers bin a resume the moment they suspect AI, before reading on [13]. What gets most resumes dropped, though, is bland writing and a claim that folds under one follow-up question.

**What’s the ideal resume length?**

One page under ten years of work, two pages beyond that, is the usual guide. Screening data shows a longer CV gets more total time, but only the first page gets a close read.
