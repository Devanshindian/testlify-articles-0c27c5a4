Hackathon Judging: The Rubric and AI-Use Policy

3,811 words · 10 sections · 9 sources

Your hackathon ends with a room full of demos and no agreed way to rank them. Two judges watch the same build and walk away with different answers, and hackathon judging has no written rule to settle it. In our research with SHRM Labs, 45.8% of HR and TA practitioners said hiring confidence varies by hiring manager or role.

Nobody wrote the AI rules down either. So two submissions land and you cannot tell what produced either one. Fix the dimensions, the weights and the AI policy before anyone starts building, and the score becomes evidence you can defend.

What counts as a hiring hackathon (and what doesn't)

A hiring hackathon is a timed work sample inside your funnel. Candidates build something real against a fixed brief, in a fixed window, and what they hand in decides whether they move forward. That consequence is what separates it from a public competition, a student event, an internal innovation day or a security challenge. Those events rank, teach or entertain; yours has to back a hiring decision.

Ask people to build real features in a realistic setup and you have built a work sample test. That helps you, and it also raises the bar. A demo day can be judged on taste. A screening step needs job-linked criteria, a written score, and evidence your hiring team can explain months later.

The payoff is that the task looks like the job. Work samples carry less adverse impact risk than most other screens (adverse impact is when a hiring step rejects one group far more often than another) 1, and they meet content-validity rules when you build them from real job tasks 1. Candidates accept them more readily too, because they can see the link to the role 1. None of that survives without scoring criteria you set in advance 1.

Decide what the hackathon score is allowed to decide

Write down what the score is allowed to decide before anyone builds. A hackathon feels objective because people ship real things, but the signal comes from your criteria, not from the demo. Keep the answer narrow: does this person move forward, and on what evidence.

Without a written hackathon judging plan, judges apply the criteria differently. A build's rating then tells you which judge watched it, not how good it was. Fix the weights and the evidence rules first and you get back fairness, speed, consistency, and trust from candidates.

The SHRM Labs number above says this is already happening. Scoring against set criteria beats gut feel every time. Splitting a vague word like quality into parts you can measure is what makes two judges' scores comparable.

Pick 3-6 job-linked dimensions and fix weights first

Build the rubric from the job description, not from what looks impressive on demo day. Name the skills the exercise is meant to test. Three to six dimensions is the range that works. More than that and judges tire, so scores drift; fewer and the rubric is too blunt to show what went wrong.

Here is the order to work in:

  1. Pull the skills from the job description. Name what the challenge tests, then check you can actually see each one in a submission.
  2. Name the dimensions. Pick from accuracy, instruction adherence, completeness, safety, reasoning quality, and tone or style. Then rename them in the words your role uses.
  3. Pick one scale and stick to it. Everything below uses 1 to 5, with a written definition at every point.
  4. Set the weights before the event, not after the demos.
  5. Write the evidence rules. Say whether judges may look past the submission, whether missing documentation costs points, and whether they can guess at intent.
  6. Settle the edge cases in writing. A build that works but ignores a stated constraint has to lose points somewhere. Say where: instruction adherence, completeness, or both.

Tight evidence rules cut arguments between judges. Point at least one dimension at reasoning, not just the thing they built, or polish will outscore thinking. Then put the task, the dimensions, the allowed evidence and your notes on close calls into one short template every judge reads. Short enough to survive a long judging day.

A weighted hackathon judging rubric you can copy

Here is the hackathon judging rubric. Copy it, swap in the dimensions your role needs, and lock the weights before anyone starts building. Most hackathons judge on the same short list: technical execution, innovation or originality, impact and usefulness, design, completeness, and presentation. The weighting is what changes, and yours should match what your hiring process actually rewards.

For an AI-build challenge, split it into evidence, feasibility and workflow fit. Novelty only counts when it serves something you could ship. Score each dimension 1-5, multiply by the weight, then add them up. Use a spreadsheet or a scoring tool, and check the totals before you rank anyone.

Most panels skip the anchors below. Telling judges to score 1 to 5 is not a rubric. Written anchors, each with a worked example, are what keep a 5 meaning the same thing to every judge.

Show judges one pre-graded example at each end of the scale before they score anything. Seeing a top and a bottom submission does more for consistency than any amount of careful wording. A plain build the candidate can explain should beat a polished one nobody can.

How to calibrate judges so scores actually agree

Judges score alone first and compare only after every candidate has been rated. That order stops one loud opinion from setting the room's baseline before anyone has read the evidence. Put at least two reviewers on each submission and hide their scores until the last candidate is done 2. Give each judge their own scoring view, not a shared sheet.

Run a short calibration pass before the event

Run calibration after the rubric is written and before live judging opens. The rubric says what good looks like. Calibration proves your panel reads that the same way. Skip it and you find the disagreement later, when it costs more.

  1. Pick samples across the whole quality range. A facilitator selects examples from weak to strong, so the panel uses the full scale.
  2. Have each judge score one or two anonymized samples alone. Old candidate work, redacted notes or seeded practice builds all work. No discussion.
  3. Walk the scale and the criteria tiers together first. Cover must-have, nice-to-have and bonus criteria. Use real examples until everyone uses the same words for the same behaviors.
  4. Put every judge's score for one sample on a single sheet. A 2 and a 5 on the same criterion is the gap you go after.
  5. Make each judge back their rating with evidence. How a score felt is not a reason. Only a specific behavior or artifact counts 3.
  6. Rewrite the vague wording, then stop. You want sharper language before live judging, not one agreed score on a practice item.

You will usually find one or two criteria that need tighter wording. One judge treats a clickable prototype as proof the thing works. Another wants evidence from the person who owns the workflow. You never see that split until the scores sit side by side.

Check agreement instead of assuming it

A rubric that reads well can still produce scattered scores. Every submission already has two scores, so pull 10-20% of those pairs and settle the disagreements instead of letting them slide. Measure the agreement: Cohen's kappa for two reviewers, Krippendorff's alpha when you have more raters or gaps in the ratings. An alpha of 0.800 or above is the usual bar, and 0.667 to 0.800 can pass for tentative calls, depending on what a wrong decision costs.

That is the strict version. In recruiting, the floor is lower: anything below 0.40 is a warning sign, tracked monthly. Telling reviewers to try harder will not close a gap that size. When two raters land more than a point apart, the rubric is the problem 3.

Adjudicate split scores, then revise the rubric

Settle a disagreement by talking it out or bringing in a third reviewer. Never average the two numbers, and never take a majority 2. Then track which criteria split your panel most often, because one that keeps dividing people is either vague or a matter of taste 2. Usually it is overlapping dimensions, where quality, impact and completeness half mean the same thing, or anchors built from adjectives instead of thresholds.

Write down every close call and the reason behind it. That log settles the next ambiguous submission and tells you what to fix in the next rubric. Keep the calibrated rubric somewhere shared, so judges check the document instead of their memory.

Calibration is not a one-off. Run the same role a few times and scores drift, either creeping up toward strong yes or bunching in the middle. Re-anchor the panel every quarter. Any judge who joins later runs a short version before scoring a live submission.

Review the borderline scores, and keep every judge's notes

Treat the rubric total as one input to a human decision, never the decision itself. One hackathon has staff read every flagged submission and every borderline case before anyone advances. Its policy says "automated scores are inputs to human judgment, not the sole determining factor" 4.

A second reader is what keeps that review from being a rubber stamp. On any borderline score, hand it to a judge who has not seen it and let them read the evidence cold. Pair the build with other formats too, rather than betting a decision on one judge's read of one demo. When two candidates finish close, that wider evidence shows you the trade-off instead of letting demo polish decide.

Ask every judge for a short reason quoting the evidence, especially on low scores. A number with no reason attached is useless later, when you want to fix an anchor or recalibrate a judge. Ask for the reasoning first and the score second. You get better scores and a trail you can show.

A record you can defend needs more than the point total. Judging tools built for sponsor disputes hand you a downloadable breakdown per judge, not one aggregate leaderboard. You see who scored what on which criterion, instead of asking the panel to remember.

Set a retention period, and tie it to a reason. One hackathon keeps qualifier scores and feedback until its appeals date closes, holds advancement decisions for the same window, then keeps anonymous score distributions for up to two years to improve the program 4. Keep your rubric scores and AI disclosures long enough to answer an appeal, or a question months later about why someone did not advance. AI hiring rules increasingly ask for a per-candidate score breakdown you can explain, and this is where it comes from.

A blanket AI ban breaks; saying nothing breaks worse

Write the AI rules before anyone builds, because both easy defaults fail. One reported policy bars candidates from using AI at any point in the application or interview, with disqualification during the process and termination if it surfaces after hire 5. Commentators call that extreme, since using AI to sharpen an application and rehearse for interviews is now a normal job-hunting skill 5. Candidates agree: a blanket ban reads as distrust, and some walk away from the employer over it 5.

Saying nothing costs more. A 2023 survey found almost half of job seekers were already using ChatGPT for resumes and cover letters, and 70% got a better response rate 6. Legal guidance notes employers often never spot it 6. Our own research with SHRM Labs found 73.4% of HR and TA practitioners are worried about candidates using AI during interviews or assessments.

Without a written policy you cannot compare two submissions fairly, because you have no idea what produced either one.

Disclosure on its own will not fix that. Across 16 studies with more than 27,000 participants, text labeled AI-assisted scored lower than identical text labeled human-written. A pre-registered survey found the same thing: people call disclosure important, then mark down the work that discloses 7. So a promise that disclosure costs nothing only holds if you tell judges exactly how to score it: allow the tool, then make the candidate defend what it produced.

The AI-use policy you can hand candidates

Your policy has to separate preparation from AI standing in for the candidate's own thinking in the moment. Polishing a resume or rehearsing for an interview is unremarkable 5. The hard line is an answer to a live, role-specific scenario that is "100% AI and not of their own thought" 5. Candidates cannot aim at that line unless you write it down before the challenge starts.

Publish the rules before anyone builds. Whatever you score a submission on had to be visible while the candidate was working. Employment lawyers say the same: any restriction on AI use needs a nondiscriminatory business reason attached in advance 6. Running more than one track? Post rules per track, because a senior engineering track and a product track restrict for different reasons 6.

Define what AI may and may not generate

Split it this way: AI as scaffolding around the candidate's own work, versus AI writing the substance for them. Boilerplate is the carve-out. Delete a block of code, and if a template off the internet replaces it in five minutes, it was boilerplate. If deleting it breaks something only your product does, that is the feature work you are actually judging.

Require a disclosure statement judges can actually score

A disclosure you can score names the tool, says what it did, and confirms a person checked the output before submission. Make it a required field in the submission form, not a line buried in terms nobody reads. Force each field instead of taking free text, because missing pieces are the top reason disclosure statements get sent back in academic settings.

Ask category by category: scaffolding, debugging, code review and documentation, with the tool named for each 9. Each one maps to a different rubric line, so judges can see where AI got involved. How much detail you need depends on how much the AI shaped things. Editing help gets one confirming line; AI that shaped core logic means naming the functions and files it touched.

Score the disclosure on those three elements, and keep it outside the weighted dimensions so your weights stay fixed. "Used AI to help" scores lower than a list of exactly where AI entered the build.

Set consequences for missing or false disclosure

Put the consequence in writing before the event: hiding AI use, or lying about it, may end the process or cost someone an offer 8. Published hiring rules pair a stage-specific ban with a plain warning, and yours should read the same way. If undisclosed use turns up later, apply the point deduction or disqualification rule you already wrote into the rubric.

Say the other half just as plainly, or candidates will read disclosure as a trap and stay quiet. One AI hackathon says it in a line: using AI tools with proper disclosure does not disadvantage your submission. Where the use is allowed, you score the execution, not the fact of disclosure. And since labeled work gets marked down by default, that promise only holds if judges use the same anchors on disclosed submissions, and if you check that they do.

How to verify a candidate actually built it

You verify in a live conversation, not with a detection tool. AI turns out polished work in almost any scenario, so the follow-up chat is what tells you whether the candidate understands what they handed in. Ask them to walk through one hard call, explain an option they rejected, and change something small on the spot. That conversation catches undisclosed or misrepresented AI use better than any compliance check.

What they wrote gives you the questions. Watch for repeated phrases, generic content that misses the actual task, patchy formatting, tone that shifts, language fancier than the task needs, and two candidates who sound alike 6. Another tell is an answer built to max out a known rubric, like complexity analysis nobody asked for on an algorithm question.

Human work usually shows the mess of getting there. AI solutions arrive finished, with no dead ends in sight. Look for signs of iteration: commit history, notes on approaches they dropped, trade-offs written down. Treat all of it as a prompt for questions, not proof, because a tidy commit history is easy to fake.

Where AI use is part of the job, spell out the boundary instead of letting candidates guess. Score the behavior, not the tool: did they prompt and iterate, did they check and debug the output, is the disclosure specific. A rubric is hard to game when every criterion has anchored bands and demands evidence.

Then watch your own judges. If one quietly marks down anything that admits AI use, your disclosure rule is dead. Every score should trace back to the evidence that produced it.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a hiring hackathon run?

Four to eight hours for a screening round, and no more than a weekend for a final stage. Longer events pick for free time rather than skill, and they shrink your candidate pool.

What is the difference between a hiring hackathon and a take-home assignment?

A hackathon is timed, so you see how someone works under pressure. A take-home is private and untimed. Neither limits AI use on its own, so both need the same rubric and the same disclosure rules.

Should you pay candidates for a hiring hackathon?

Pay for anything past a few hours, and for any work you could actually ship. Only a short round goes unpaid, so a full-day build does not qualify. Prizes are no substitute; only winners get one.

Do hiring hackathons work for non-technical roles?

Yes, when the brief is a work product: a campaign plan, a support escalation, a pricing case. Score it with the same weighted rubric. The format is the timing, not the code.

How do you give feedback to candidates who do not advance after a hackathon?

Send the rubric dimensions and where they fell short, taken from the judges' written reasons. Keep it about behavior. Do not rank them against other candidates or hint at a future round.

A hackathon only screens when the rubric, the weights and the AI rules are fixed before the first commit. Everything after that is bookkeeping: private scores, a calibration pass, and a written reason behind every number.
