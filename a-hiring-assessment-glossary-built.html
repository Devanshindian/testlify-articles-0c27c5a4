A Skills Assessment Glossary for Recruiters Running Tests

2,450 words · 9 sections · 4 sources

You pick a skills assessment. You send it to forty applicants. By Friday you are looking at a column of numbers that decides who gets a phone call. Nobody handed you the words that came with the test. Validity. Norm group. Cut score. Adverse impact. Psychologists wrote those words for other psychologists. Now they are settings on your screen.

Get one of them slightly wrong and it costs you, quietly. A recruiter reads a percentile as a percentage. She decides 70 looks like a weak pass. She rejects a candidate who beat seven applicants out of ten.

Quick answer

A skills assessment is a scored test. You set it up, send it out, and may later have to defend it. Pick a test type that matches the two or three abilities the job turns on, and send it right after the application. Do not ask a vendor whether the test is validated. Ask which study covers your job.

Then set the pass mark from your own pool size and your own performance data. Before it goes live, check the pass rates it produces across demographic groups.

What a Skills Assessment Is When You Run It

A skills assessment is a test you send before an offer. It tells you whether someone can do the job. Every candidate gets the same test and the same marking. That sameness is what lets you explain a rejection six months later.

Teams add a test because resume sorting is a private judgment nobody checks. A recruiter skims sixty applications on a Tuesday afternoon and forms an opinion, with no record of what she weighed. Companies that put a validated assessment in front of that step cut turnover by 65% on average, against teams working from resumes and interviews alone. Turnover is the number that moves, because the test catches the mismatch before the hire, not six months into it.

The Five Test Types, and What Each One Predicts

Five kinds of test are proven to predict how someone will do at work: cognitive ability, skills or job knowledge, personality, situational judgment, and work samples. Each catches a different way a hire goes wrong. Use two or three per role, not all five.

Test typeThe failure it catches
Cognitive abilityCannot pick up an unfamiliar problem
Skills or job knowledgeCannot yet do the task the role is paid for
Work sample or simulationInterviews well and produces poorly
Situational judgmentMakes the wrong call in an ordinary moment
Personality and behavioralWorking style will not survive the team

Cognitive ability predicts best on its own, across the widest range of jobs. So if you can only add one, add that one.

Start with the job, not the test. Write down the two or three skills the hire cannot fail at in the first ninety days, then pick the test that measures those. A developer gets a code review. A support hire gets a scenario about a refund that never arrived.

The forty-minute ceiling, and what drop-off costs you

Where the test sits decides how many people ever take it. Put it at step two, right after the application and before anyone reads a resume. The scores then build your shortlist instead of thinning it three weeks later.

Now watch the clock. Criteria looked at roughly half a million assessments. Up to about forty minutes, close to 80% of candidates finish. Past an hour of total testing, that falls to around 60%. Every candidate who quits halfway is one you never scored, and the ones who walk away have somewhere else to be, holding a competing offer.

So keep the screening stage to forty minutes. If the role needs more depth, add a second short assessment after the shortlist.

Validity: What a Vendor Is Claiming When They Say Validated

Validity is not something the test owns. It sits in how you read the scores and what you do because of them. The thing you would have to defend is your decision to screen someone out at a given score.

The kind that settles the argument is criterion validity. It asks one blunt question. Do the people who scored higher actually do the job better? A vendor proves it by lining scores up against performance data: manager ratings, sales per hour, whether the person stayed a year.

The word validated on a pricing page does not mean any of that. A vendor can honestly say a test has been validated in accordance with the Uniform Guidelines. One legal analysis calls that sentence irrelevant at best and potentially misleading 1.

So ask for the technical manual, and ask for the psychologist on staff rather than the account executive. Almost nobody does. In our own survey with SHRM Labs of 128 practitioners, 50.8% had no internal check on assessment validity at all.

Validated for whose job? The call centre problem

Unless a test has been validated for your specific job, it is not valid for that job 1. Evidence gathered at other companies, in other industries, does nothing for you.

The trap is two jobs with the same title. Picture two call centre roles advertised in almost the same words. One is inbound: the agent takes the call and pulls up account data while the customer waits. The other is outbound sales, and that person keeps dialling after nine people say no. Persistence carries the second job. In the first it barely matters.

A test validated on the inbound team will not defend a rejection on the outbound one. You have swapped a judgment nobody wrote down for a written rule you cannot explain.

Reliability, and Why a Score Is a Range Not a Number

Reliability asks an easier question. If the same person took this test again next month, would they land on roughly the same score? When the answer is no, nothing else about the test matters. You are not measuring a person. You are measuring a mood.

Vendors report it as a number that tops out at 1.00, meaning perfect consistency. There is an accepted floor, and below it the estimate is too weak to build a decision on. What you want is a number well clear of that floor.

So treat a score as a range, not a point. A 68 and a 72 can be the same candidate on two different mornings, one of them after a bad night.

Norm Groups: Why the Same Score Lands at Two Percentiles

A raw score is how many questions the candidate got right. A percentile rank is a position. It tells you what share of a comparison group scored the same or lower.

This is where it gets expensive. On a hard test, a candidate at the 70th percentile may have answered fewer than 60% of the questions correctly. A recruiter who reads that 70 as a percentage sees a weak pass, and rejects someone who outperformed seven applicants in ten.

The comparison group changes the result as much as the answers do. A raw score of 28 out of 40 sits at the 80th percentile against general population norms, and at the 55th against graduate norms. Same person, same answers, two verdicts. So the question is what they scored compared to whom.

Cut Score: How to Set the Line You Screen On

A cut score is the line that splits pass from fail. A person sets it. It is not in the data, because no test has a natural border between competent and not competent.

So start from your numbers. Say you need 10 hires and expect 80 candidates. A cut at the 90th percentile passes roughly eight people, before drop-off, before interview rejections, before the two who take other offers. Work backwards: pool size, hire count, the pass rate that leaves a workable shortlist, and only then a score.

Then check that line against people you already trust. Pull ninety-day performance data on your current team and find where the strong ones cluster. If they sit between the 72nd and 88th percentile, the evidence points to a cut near the 70th. Not the arbitrary 75th somebody wrote into the policy three years ago.

Adverse Impact and the Four-Fifths Rule

Adverse impact is what happens when one standard, applied to everybody the same way, still produces very different pass rates across demographic groups. It is about your outcome, not anyone's intent, and you measure it on your own hiring data.

The four-fifths rule puts a number on it. Take the selection rate for any race, sex, or ethnic group and compare it with the rate for the highest group. If it falls below four-fifths of that rate, federal enforcement agencies will generally regard it as evidence of adverse impact.

Here is the arithmetic on a scored test for entry-level engineers. The company hires 30 of 50 women, a 60% rate, and 80 of 100 men, an 80% rate. Divide 60 by 80 and you get 75%, under the threshold, so the rule is violated 2. Nobody in that hiring loop did a single deliberate thing. The cut score did it.

Which makes the cut score your lever on this ratio. Ask any provider for pass rate data broken down by group at the score you are considering. A provider who cannot produce it has told you something.

Accommodations, and When a Test Is a Pre-Employment Medical Exam

A reasonable accommodation is any change to your hiring process, or to the way the test normally runs, that lets a qualified candidate with a disability show what they can actually do. Extra time is the common one, along with a question read aloud when a screen reader will not talk to your platform.

Here is the part teams miss. A request rarely announces itself. A candidate never has to say disability, accommodation, or ADA. An email asking for more time to complete the pre-interview online assessment is a request, and your exposure begins the moment a coordinator reads it as a scheduling question.

When a personality test counts as a medical examination

Before you extend an offer, the ADA bars disability-related inquiries and medical examinations. That ban reaches tests nobody on your team would file under medical. Personality tests are named directly among the things you cannot require before a conditional offer.

The case to know is Karraker v. Rent-A-Center. The company made management candidates sit the MMPI, used the vendor's vocational scoring rather than the clinical one, and had no psychologist read the results. The court held that none of it mattered. However the test is scored, it hurts candidates with mental disorders, so it is best categorized as a medical examination 3.

Read that as a rule about placement. A personality test that can surface a mental health condition belongs after a conditional offer, run the same way for every entering candidate in that job category.

What a Proctoring Flag Is Worth, and What It Isn't

Proctoring means watching a candidate while they sit the test, and a remote system does it through the webcam, the microphone, and the screen. It looks for a second monitor, a phone, a copied question, another person in the room. What it cannot do is judge whether any of that mattered.

So a flag is evidence for a person to review, never a verdict. Automated proctoring handles the most volume and misfires most, because an algorithm that scores suspicion produces a lot of false positives. Looking away to think. A housemate crossing the frame. A connection dropping for eight seconds. All of it trips the same wire.

The misfires are not spread evenly either. The University of Louisville studied roughly 357 students. Candidates with darker skin tones were significantly more likely to be marked as needing review, and women with the darkest skin tones were flagged far more often than anyone else 4. Act on flags without a human reading them and that becomes your rejection pattern.

Write the review policy before launch: who looks at a flag, inside what window, and what justifies acting on one. Decide it later and you will decide it under pressure, with a live candidate waiting.

Frequently asked questions

What is involved in a skills assessment?

Three things. A short timed test of the abilities the role needs, automatic scoring against a comparison group, and a pass mark you set in advance. Keep the whole thing under forty minutes.

Can you give me an example of a skills assessment?

A support candidate sorting a messy spreadsheet in ten minutes. A developer reviewing a pull request. A written scenario asking how they would answer an angry customer.

What is the difference between a skills assessment and an aptitude test?

A skills test measures what someone can already do, like Excel or SQL. An aptitude or cognitive test measures how fast they pick up something new. Use aptitude for trainees, skills for defined roles.

Can a candidate retake a skills assessment they failed?

Only if the retake rule is written down and applied to everybody the same way. Set a waiting period and use a different set of questions, because a second sitting on the same items measures memory.

Do I have to tell candidates the assessment is being recorded?

Yes, and before they start: what is watched, what is stored, and for how long. Illinois regulates biometric data, and New York City requires notice for automated hiring tools.
