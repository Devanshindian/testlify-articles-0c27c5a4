# Cost Per Hire in 2026: $1,500 to $250K, Not $4,129

The cost per hire number in your deck is probably $4,129. It comes from a [SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat) survey run in 2016, and it is the smaller half of the bill.

Recruiter hours, interview panels and the empty seat cost more than every invoice combined. Nobody bills them. Counted properly, one hire runs from about $1,500 to more than $250,000, depending on the role and how many people you hire. The number you can defend is a cost line per role and per volume, with its source and edition attached.

## Quick answer

Cost per hire is your total recruiting spend divided by the hires it produced. The version most teams quote comes from a SHRM edition that has since been replaced, and it counts only the billed part: job ads, agency fees, checks, software. About 60 percent of what a hire costs is recruiter and hiring manager time, plus the work nobody did while the seat sat empty. None of that reaches a purchase order.

So one blended average will not hold up. Quote the current figure with its edition and sample attached. Then split your own number by role band, and by how many hires the fixed spend covers.

## ## $4,700 Is a 2022 Figure. $4,129 Is From a 2016 Survey

The $4,700 average most decks still quote comes from a SHRM report published April 11, 2022, and SHRM has moved past it. Its 2025 release says the new study "updates SHRM's prior benchmarking studies from 2022, reflecting the latest trends and priorities in the HR landscape" [1]. The figure was right when it landed. It is four years old now.

Underneath it sits $4,129, older still and quoted everywhere. That one is SHRM's Human Capital Benchmarking Report for fiscal 2015, published August 2016, from 2,048 members surveyed February to April that year. One current SHRM page now files it as a legacy figure, separate from the 2022 study and the newer releases.

So what gets quoted is not one stale number. It is a stack of editions, each cited as if it were the present one. They do not compare with each other either: respondents can skip metrics, so the sample behind a single figure varies within one edition [1]. Lift a number out of its edition, its averaging method and its sample, and it is too loose to build a budget on.

## ## Average or Median? $5,475, $10,600 and $1,300

SHRM publishes two kinds of number, and they are not interchangeable. Its medians and its averages come from separate surveys with separate respondent pools, so the same metric lands about 4x apart for non-executive roles [1]. SHRM reports medians in its recruiting series on purpose: to stop extreme values skewing the result, and to keep years comparable.

| Figure | SHRM publication and year | Average or median | Sample size and data window | Why it does not compare directly with the others |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $5,475 non-executive | 2025 SHRM Benchmarking Report, released October 15, 2025 [1] | Average [1] | 2,371 respondents, surveyed January 9 to March 3, 2025, oversampled for vice president and above [1] | Average from the benchmarking series; a few costly hires pull it up  |
| $35,879 executive | 2025 SHRM Benchmarking Report, released October 15, 2025  | Average [1] | 2,371 respondents, surveyed January 9 to March 3, 2025 [1] | Same survey as the row above, so it compares to that one and to no median [1] |
| $10,600 executive | 2026 Recruiting Executives Benchmarking Report, trend table  | Median  | Over 4,600 organizations, data collected through November 24, 2025  | Roughly the same period as the $35,879 average, but less than half the size  |
| $1,300 non-executive | 2026 Recruiting Executives Benchmarking Report  | Median  | 4,657 respondents, data collected through November 24, 2025  | Different survey, different pool, and a midpoint rather than an average  |
| Approximately $4,700 | The Real Costs of Recruitment, April 11, 2022 [2] | Average [2] | - | No sample size published, and no separate executive figure [2] |

Read across a row, never down a column. A board paper that sets an average against someone else's median looks wrong even when every figure in it is quoted correctly.

## ## The SHRM Formula, and Where It Stops Counting

Whichever figure you cite, it was built the same way. [Cost per hire](https://testlify.com/hr-glossary/cost-per-hire-cph/) is total recruiting spend divided by the hires it produced, and under the SHRM standard it stops the moment a candidate accepts the offer. The formula SHRM treats as the industry standard is short: internal recruiting costs plus external recruiting costs, divided by total hires in the period. Spend $55,000 across 15 hires in a quarter and the figure is $3,667.

Onboarding sits outside it. So does the cost of the seat while it stayed empty. That boundary matters more than the arithmetic, because every number that follows is either inside the numerator or excluded by design.

### What Sits Inside Each Half of the Numerator

Internal costs cover the recruiting function itself. That means recruiter pay and overhead, HR admin time, tracking and sourcing software, referral bonuses, and interview hours from managers and panels. That last line is the one most often understated.

External costs are the invoiced half: job board posts, recruitment advertising, agency or retained search fees, background checks and assessments, sign-on bonuses, and relocation.

## ## Hard Costs Are Only 30 to 40% of a Hire's Total

The invoiced half is the smaller one. [Hard costs](https://testlify.com/direct-vs-indirect-hiring-costs-whats-overlooked/) are roughly 30-40% of what a hire actually costs, and the uninvoiced time of recruiters and managers accounts for the other 60% [2]. That split comes from SHRM itself. Edie [Goldberg](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/real-costs-recruitment), SHRM Foundation chair-elect, puts it as "30 percent to 40 percent are hard costs, and the other 60 percent are soft costs" [2].

The difference between the two halves is visibility, not size. Hard costs land on invoices, contracts and card statements, so finance can track every dollar. Soft costs are buried in salaries and calendars you were already paying for, so nothing bills them separately. That is why almost every published cost-per-hire figure counts the invoiced half and stops there.

### What the Invoiced Third Actually Buys

The invoiced half is short enough to list in full. Job advertising, agency and search fees, background checks, pre-employment assessments, recruiting software, relocation and signing bonuses cover nearly all of it. Each one attaches to a named role and a named hiring cycle, which is why it forecasts cleanly.

Priced one by one, most of these are small. A LinkedIn contract job posting runs $200-$1,000 per month per slot. Background checks start near $3 for a minimal criminal-record search, and skills assessments run about $50 per candidate. An applicant tracking system (ATS), the software that moves candidates through the pipeline, spreads across a year's hiring at about $50 per hire.

Two lines break the pattern. Agency fees scale with salary instead of sitting at a fixed price, and relocation averages $21,792 for a renter moving within the country. Both attach to one hire rather than to hiring in general. So the hard-cost total swings wide between a junior and a senior role.

Add the small lines up and a standard role comes to a few thousand dollars. That is auditable and defensible. It is also, by SHRM's own ratio, about a third of the bill.

## ## Agency Recruitment Fees: 15-25% Contingency, 30-35% Retained

Agency fees are the line that breaks the pattern. For most professional or senior roles they are the largest external cost you carry. [Contingency search](https://testlify.com/hr-glossary/contingency-recruitment/), the model used for most mid-level hiring, runs 15-25% of the hire's first-year salary, and you pay only on placement.

Retained executive search sits higher, around 30-35% of salary, paid in installments as stages of the search complete. Container search splits the difference at 25% to 33% of first-year pay, part of it upfront to buy dedicated recruiter time.

The difference is commitment, not price. Contingency lets you run several firms at once and pay only the winner, which buys competition but not attention. Retained is exclusive, so the firm can put its hours into one search.

What makes the percentage bite is the salary it multiplies. On a $75,000 hire, a 20% fee is $15,000. Run the same structure on a $200,000 executive and a 30% fee is $60,000, more than several junior hires cost together. Fees climb with scarcity too, so technology, financial and health sciences searches carry a premium.

### In-House or Agency: The Breakeven Sits Near 15 Hires a Year

Volume decides this, not preference. An in-house recruiting team costs $146,000-$200,000 a year, fixed whether it fills five roles or 50. Agency spend is purely variable, so the breakeven lands around 15-20 hires a year. Below that line, agencies are cheaper.

Speed complicates it. Agencies fill roles in about 42 days against 63 for in-house teams. The vacancy you pay for is shorter, even when the invoice is bigger. That gap matters most on the roles where the fee is highest.

### The $2,000 Referral That Replaces a $15,000 Fee

Referrals undercut both models. The average employee referral bonus is $2,500. On a $100,000 role, a $2,000 bonus beats a 20% fee every time, and referred hires stay longer.

Two things kill referral programs, and neither is the bonus size. One is slow payment, the other a painful submission process. Pay within 30 days of the start date and keep the nomination to a single form.

## ## The Uninvoiced 60%: 23 Interview Hours, a $22,000 Vacancy

Everything priced so far arrives on an invoice. The other 60% does not. Interviewer hours, recruiter screening time and the output lost to an empty seat reach no purchase order. That makes them unbudgeted, not small.

Most cost-per-hire figures leave them out: manager time, no-show interviews, and the work nobody does while a seat is open. Onboarding sits in the same blind spot at $1,500 to $5,000 per employee once you count orientation, mentoring time and initial processing. None of this is guesswork. It is paid in salary rather than invoices, so it lands in a different budget and gets attributed to nobody.

### Interview Hours Are the Biggest Unbilled Line on the Sheet

Interview time is the single largest hidden cost in a hire. A technical hire now averages 23.3 interview hours against 12.2 for a business hire. By function the spread is wider: data roles top the chart at 24.9 hours, customer support at 8.9 [3]. Those hours belong to senior people you already pay well, so at any credible blended rate the interview line alone rivals a mid-sized agency fee.

Round count drives that number, and you choose it. Technical roles now average 17.6 interviews per hire, up 52% from roughly 11 in 2021. Ask enough hiring teams why, and the answer is rarely rigor: rounds get added when the panel cannot agree on its own criteria. The same senior role runs at two rounds in one company and six in another.

### Applications Per Hire Tripled While Talent Acquisition Headcount Fell 14 Percent

Volume is the second unbilled line, and it moved fast. The average recruiter now processes 291 applications per hire, up from roughly 100 in early 2021 [3]. Talent acquisition teams are working with about 14% less headcount than they had in the last hiring boom. More screening, fewer screeners, no change to any invoice.

Recruiters reckon two to six percent of that volume is worth a serious look. Many have stopped posting roles and gone back to direct outreach. That costs more per candidate and is harder to spread across a batch of hires. It is also time not spent closing candidates, which is the work that actually pulls cost per hire down.

A measurement gap sits under all of it. Our SHRM Labs research with Testlify found a quarter of HR and talent acquisition practitioners had never measured recruiter time on screening versus strategic work. You cannot defend a cost line you have never counted.

### The Empty Seat You Are Paying For While the Role Stays Open

[Vacancy cost](https://testlify.com/real-cost-of-a-slow-hiring-process/) sits outside the SHRM/ANSI standard, because that standard stops at the accepted offer. The work does not stop with it. It piles up, gets pushed onto teammates already at capacity, or goes undone. All three cost you.

Put the standard figures together. Lost output for an average professional role runs about $500 a day, and median time to fill in 2026 is 63.5 days. At 44 working days, that is $22,000 gone before a single recruiting expense is counted. For a senior sales role losing $2,500 a day in pipeline, 45 days produces $112,500.

That is what the blended average cannot survive. A few thousand dollars per hire is not wrong, it is partial, and the missing part is bigger than the reported one.

## ## Cost Per Hire by Role: $1,500 Entry to $250K Exec

Even counted properly, cost per hire is not one number. It moves in bands by seniority, and the bands are wide enough that a blended average hides the figure that matters for your open role. Entry-level hiring benchmarks at $1,500 to $3,500; a [retained executive search](https://testlify.com/recruitment-vs-executive-search/) pushes the cost to between $50,000 and $250,000. Here is the published picture, band by band.

## ## Why 8 Hires a Year Cost More Per Hire Than 500

Seniority is one of the two things a blended average flattens. Volume is the other. Divide the same recruiting spend across eight hires instead of 500 and the cost per hire moves before a single role changes. Part of that spend is fixed; the number of hires it splits across is not.

Take a 40-person employer filling eight roles in a quarter. Job board posts, two background checks and one agency fee come to $6,000 in external cost. Recruiter and hiring manager hours on resumes, interviews and scheduling add $9,000. Divide $15,000 by eight and you get $1,875 per hire.

Now run it at scale. A 200-person employer making 40 hires a year carries $70,000 of internal cost, including a recruiter salary allocation and a prorated ATS subscription. Add $50,000 in job boards, agency placements, checks and referral bonuses. That is $3,000 per hire.

The two figures do not compare with each other. One covers a quarter, the other a year, which is exactly the mismatched-period error set out below. Each just shows the formula run at its own scale.

Smaller employers still pay more because their fixed costs do not shrink to match the smaller denominator. With no recruiting team of their own, they lean harder on agencies and paid postings for each opening. That costs less in total at their volume, and more per hire.

The benchmarks bear it out: companies under 50 employees often run past $7,500 per hire, against a US average nearer $4,750. That figure uses a different source and method than the SHRM averages above, so it is not a like-for-like comparison.

One model runs the other way on purpose. Flat-rate hiring events charge a fixed price however many hires come out, so cost per hire falls as volume rises. Across 582 healthcare hiring events in 2025, employers averaged eight hires each, under $200 per hire at the top pricing tier before staff time. It only works for multi-role, hourly and clinical hiring, where interview volume is the constraint.

## ## What Cost Per Hire Misses: Time to Fill and Quality of Hire

Four calculation errors will undo a per-role, per-volume cost line, and all four are avoidable. The first is a mismatched period: annual recruiting spend divided by a monthly or quarterly hire count is wrong however carefully you sourced the spend. The second is fractional counting. Hires go into the numerator as whole hires, not as full-time-equivalents.

The third is comparing across companies with no controls. Industry, role mix, geography and method all move the number, so a rival's published figure is not a benchmark you can hold yourself against. The fourth is comparing inside your own data without segmenting first. Run a bulk individual-contributor push and one specialized search through a single blended average, and you misstate both.

Then there is the failure no formula catches. You can lower cost per hire by lowering the bar. The metric counts effort and speed, not outcome, so the number improves right up until the new hire underperforms or quits.

So never send it out alone. Time to fill counts calendar days from open requisition to accepted offer, and confirms the process improved. Quality of hire, built from 90-day retention and early manager performance scores, shows whether the saving was real or just deferred.

Only 20% of organizations track quality of hire alongside cost per hire [1]. Run all three and you are defending a metric set, not one average someone can pick apart.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the average cost per hire in the US?**

SHRM's 2025 benchmarking average is $5,475 for a non-executive hire and $35,879 for an executive [1]. Both are averages from one survey of 2,371 respondents, so a few costly hires pull them up.

**Does cost per hire include onboarding?**

No. The SHRM standard stops at the accepted offer, so onboarding sits outside it. Budget it separately at $1,500 to $5,000 per employee, covering orientation, mentoring time and initial processing.

**Should recruiter salaries be included in cost per hire?**

Yes. Recruiter pay and overhead count as internal recruiting costs under the SHRM formula. So do HR admin time, your applicant tracking system, referral bonuses and hiring manager interview hours.

**How often should we calculate cost per hire?**

Quarterly if you hire steadily, annually if you hire in bursts. Match the spend window to the hire count window. Annual spend divided by a quarterly hire count gives a wrong figure every time.

**How much does a $20 an hour employee cost an employer?**

About $52,000 to $58,000 a year. A $20 hourly rate is $41,600 in base pay, and payroll taxes, benefits and insurance usually add 25% to 40% on top. Cost per hire is separate, and one-off.
