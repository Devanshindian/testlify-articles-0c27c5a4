# The Real Cost Per Hire in 2026: What $4,129 Leaves Out

Most hiring budgets defended this year lean on a cost per hire that is older than the plan it supports. The $4,129 still quoted in board decks came from a survey the Society for Human Resource Management ([SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat)) ran in spring 2016. Age is the smaller problem.

Built the way most teams build it, that number counts only what a vendor invoiced you, and invoices are well under half the real total. It also stops at the accepted offer, so an empty seat draining $500 a day never enters it. A figure you can defend names its edition, its averaging method, and the role band and hiring volume behind it.

## Quick answer

Cost per hire is all your internal and external recruiting spend for a period, divided by the hires you made in it. The number most budgets still quote is about a decade old, and its publisher has retired it. Before you quote a newer one, check whether it is an average or a median. The current SHRM figures differ by thousands of dollars for the same kind of role.

Invoiced costs are only 30 to 40% of what a hire really costs. Interview hours, onboarding and the empty seat carry the rest. Split your own number by role band and by hiring volume before you compare it to any benchmark.

## The $4,129 Figure Was Collected in Spring 2016

Both numbers people use to defend a hiring budget are out of date, and SHRM has already replaced them. The $4,129 comes from SHRM's Human Capital Benchmarking Report for fiscal year 2015, published in August 2016. Its data came from 2,048 SHRM members surveyed between February and April of that year.

The newer figure has the same problem in a shorter time frame. The $4,700 in circulation today comes from SHRM's report The Real Costs of Recruitment, published April 11, 2022. SHRM's own 2025 release says the new study "updates SHRM's prior benchmarking studies from 2022, reflecting the latest trends and priorities in the HR field" [1]. That is the publisher retiring its own number, in writing.

So you are not dealing with one stale statistic. You are dealing with a stack of editions, each still quoted as though it were current, none of them carrying a date when it lands on a slide.

Neither figure was wrong when it was published. But a number lifted away from its edition, its averaging method and its sample cannot anchor a 2026 budget.

## The $5,475 vs $1,300 Split: Averages vs Medians

Both current SHRM figures are right. The 2025 Benchmarking Report gives an average nonexecutive cost per hire of $5,475 [1]. The 2026 [Recruiting Executives Benchmarking](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/recruiting-benchmarking) Report gives a median of $1,300 for the same kind of role. That gap is not a correction or a market swing. It is an average sitting next to a median, from a different survey over a different window.

It matters because SHRM changed which one it publishes. An average adds every reported figure and divides by the count, so a handful of $200,000 executive searches drag it up. A median takes the middle value and ignores how extreme the tails are. SHRM says it uses medians in the 2026 dataset to avoid distortion from very high or very low values and to keep years comparable.

So the average tells you what you spend per hire overall, and the median tells you what a typical hire costs. Quote one on a board slide while finance checks the other and the meeting goes badly.

| Figure | SHRM report | Average or median | Sample and window | Why it will not compare to the row above |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $5,475 nonexecutive [1] | 2025 Benchmarking Report, October 15, 2025  | Average [1] | 2,371 respondents, fielded January 9 to March 3, 2025, oversampled for vice president and above [1] | First current row |
| $35,879 executive  | 2025 Benchmarking Report, October 15, 2025  | Average  | 2,371 respondents, January 9 to March 3, 2025 [1] | Same survey, higher role tier: nearly 7x the nonexecutive average  |
| $1,300 nonexecutive  | 2026 Recruiting Executives Benchmarking Report [2] | Median  | Over 4,600 organizations, collected through November 24, 2025  | A median against an average, from a different survey |
| $15,000 executive, 2026  | 2026 Recruiting Executives Benchmarking Report [2] | Median  | Over 4,600 organizations, through November 24, 2025  | A median, so it leaves out the very large searches that lift the $35,879 average [1] |
| $10,600 executive, 2025  | 2026 report trend table, 2017 to 2026 series [2] | Median  | Over 4,600 organizations; back-year window not stated | Same period as the $35,879 average, less than a third of it  |
| $8,800 executive, 2022  | 2026 report trend table [2] | Median  | - | Earliest median quoted here; no 2022 nonexecutive median reported  |

Pick one series and stay inside it. A 2025 average set against a 2026 median produces a contradiction that is nobody's error, and the blended "2022 number" has trained people to make it.

## The SHRM Formula, and Where It Stops Counting

Whichever series you quote, the number under it is built the same way. Cost per hire is [total internal recruiting costs](https://testlify.com/hr-glossary/cost-per-hire-cph/) plus total external recruiting costs, divided by the hires made in the same period. SHRM treats that formula as the industry standard, and the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) certified it.

Two things make it hold up in a board room. The cost lines are named, and the boundary is fixed.

- **Recruiter pay and HR admin time:** the fully loaded cost of the talent acquisition (TA) team, plus coordinator scheduling, system updates and offer paperwork.
- **Hiring manager and interviewer hours:** panel time and debriefs at a loaded hourly rate. This is the internal line teams understate most.
- **Recruiting technology and referral bonuses:** Applicant Tracking System (ATS) subscriptions, sourcing tools, assessment platforms, and what you pay out on a successful referral.
- **Job ads:** per-posting fees, job board subscriptions and paid recruitment advertising.
- **Agency and retained search fees:** a percentage of first-year salary, contingency or retained.
- **Checks, assessments, sign-on bonuses and relocation:** screening priced per candidate, plus whatever it took to close the offer.

Run it once. A mid-sized company making 15 hires in a quarter with $30,000 internal and $25,000 external lands at $3,667 per hire for that quarter's role mix.

Now the boundary. The count stops at the accepted offer. Everything after that signature, and everything the empty seat cost before it, sits outside the number.

## Hard Costs Are Only 30 to 40% of a Hire's Real Cost

[Invoiced hard costs](https://testlify.com/direct-vs-indirect-hiring-costs-whats-overlooked/) are 30% to 40% of what a hire actually costs. The soft costs nobody bills are the rest. That proportion comes from SHRM Foundation chair-elect Edie Goldberg, and it settles what the formula leaves open: build your number from invoices alone and you have captured well under half the spend.

The difference between the two halves is visibility, not importance. Hard costs sit on invoices, contracts and finance reports, so a controller can trace every dollar. Soft costs are buried in salaries and calendars you already pay for, which is why they resist counting rather than disappearing. Price the visible half exactly first.

### Everything on the invoice: ads, checks, software, relocation

These lines attach to one hire and one recruitment cycle, so they are the easiest part of the budget to forecast. Ranges move with your market and the seniority of the role, so test them against your own invoices rather than reading them as targets.

- Job ads: LinkedIn contract postings run $200-$1,000 a month per slot, and ZipRecruiter Standard plans run $299-$399 a month per job slot. Posting across three boards usually pushes monthly ad spend to $800-$1,500.
- Background checks: basic screening runs $20-$40 and a comprehensive check $100 or more, with employment or education verification adding $15-$25 per item.
- Skills assessments: roughly $50 per candidate, or a few hundred dollars a month on a bulk subscription.
- Recruiting software: ATS subscriptions run $100 to more than $1,000 a month depending on team size and features. This one is fixed, so spread it across every hire in the period.
- Relocation and signing bonuses: domestic relocation averages $21,792 for renters and $63,685 for homeowners. A signing bonus is a one-time payment tied to the offer, so it belongs here, not in payroll.

Total those five lines and you have a number you can defend to the dollar against your own finance reports. You also have, by the proportion above, about a third of what the hire will cost. The rest is hours you are already paying for.

## Cost Per Hire by Role Band: $1,500 to $250,000

Both halves of that number move with the role. Cost per hire runs in bands, from roughly $1,500 at entry level to $250,000 for a retained executive search. So a single blended average is the wrong budget line for any specific role.

Your own recruiters will say so the moment a company-wide figure hits a slide. A technical req with five qualified people alive in the market and a warehouse req that closes in a month cannot honestly share one number. Here is the same benchmark, split into bands you can test against your own invoices one at a time.

| Role band | Typical cost per hire | What drives the hard cost | What drives the soft cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entry level | $1,500-$3,500  | High-volume sourcing and job board spend  | Lighter interviews, but skills training to reach productivity  |
| Mid-level professional | $3,500-$8,000  | Standard interview loops; a mix of agency and direct hires  | Moderate training plus onboarding to company tools and culture  |
| Engineering and technical | $6,000-$23,000  | Full onsite panels; scarce skills push teams onto paid channels and agencies  | More recruiter hours sourcing per hire when talent is scarce  |
| Senior management | $10,000-$30,000  | Executive search fees; a longer process  | More rounds and longer sourcing, so more hiring-manager time  |
| Executive, retained search | $50,000-$250,000  | Retained fees at 30-35% of first-year salary  | Structured integration: stakeholder meetings, strategy briefings, relocation, coaching  |

### Why a VP hire costs several times an individual contributor

Three things move together as you climb the bands, and only one of them shows up on an invoice.

Interview intensity is the first. Senior and specialized roles run more rounds and longer sourcing cycles, and every round bills recruiter and hiring-manager time that no vendor ever charges you for.

Scarcity is the second, and it works on its own, whatever the seniority. When qualified people are thin on the ground, recruiters spend more hours per hire and teams lean on paid channels and agencies, so the internal and external lines rise together. That is why an engineering band reaches $23,000 while sitting below a director on the org chart.

Onboarding load is the third, and it inverts. Entry-level hires need skills training to get productive, and executives skip skills training almost entirely. What executives get instead is stakeholder meetings, strategy briefings, relocation support and coaching.

The spend does not vanish at the top of the band. It changes shape, moves off the recruiting budget, and stops being counted.

## Hiring Volume, Not Headcount, Sets Your Cost Per Hire

Role band is one axis of the number. Volume is the other: [cost per hire](https://testlify.com/cost-per-hire-calculator/) moves with the number of roles you fill, not the number of people you employ.

Part of recruiting spend is fixed whatever happens. ATS licenses, recruiter salaries, employer branding. Divide that base by 500 hires and it disappears into the per-hire figure. Divide it by five and it is the whole figure.

That mechanic explains an apparent contradiction in the benchmark data. Smaller employers are generally reported to pay more per hire, yet the worked examples run the other way. A 40-person company filling eight roles in a quarter, with $6,000 external and $9,000 internal, lands at $1,875 per hire. A larger organization spending $175,000 across 50 hires lands at $3,500.

Both are correct, and neither is about headcount. The 40-person company sits low because it is filling cheaper roles, not because it is efficient. Small employers pay more per hire when they have no recruiting team and lean on agencies or job board postings instead. Hold the role mix steady and the smaller denominator always wins the wrong way.

So read the number against your hiring plan, not your size band.

One channel flips the relationship on purpose. Flat-rate hiring events charge a fixed price while the number of hires produced varies, so cost per hire falls as volume rises. Across 582 healthcare hiring events in 2025, employers averaged eight hires each, working out to under $200 per hire at the top pricing tier before staff time. It only holds for multi-role, hourly and clinical hiring where interview volume is the bottleneck.

## How Much Do Recruitment Agencies Charge? 15% to 35%

Agencies are the channel low-volume employers lean on, and they are the single largest external cost on any professional or senior hire. Nothing else on the invoice comes close. The percentage is what hides the size of it.

On a $200,000 executive hire, the same logic produces a fee of $40,000 to $60,000. That is one line item, from one vendor, before an hour of your team's time is counted.

### Contingency, retained and container: what each one costs you

The structure you sign sets both the rate and when you pay it.

- **Contingency:** 15-25% of first-year salary, payable only when a candidate accepts. You can run several firms at once, but each gives your search less attention and thinner reach into people who are not actively looking.
- **Container:** 25% to 33% of first-year compensation, part paid upfront to buy dedicated recruiter time, the balance on placement. Normally director and VP searches, where you want exclusivity without a full retained budget.
- **Retained:** around 30-35% of first-year salary, sometimes plus expenses, paid in installments as stages of the search complete. It is exclusive, so the firm commits time to one client.

Two details change the total after you have agreed the rate. [Retained fees](https://testlify.com/hr-glossary/executive-search/) are usually calculated on first-year total compensation, so salary plus bonus plus equity, not base alone. And executive engagements add administrative charges of 10%-15%, plus travel, assessments and advertising the vacancy, which can add another 5%-15% on top.

### The 15 to 20 hire mark where an in-house team starts beating the fee

Breakeven sits at roughly 15-20 hires a year. Below that line, agencies win on pure arithmetic: their cost is variable and vanishes in a quiet quarter. A recruiting team costs $146,000-$200,000 a year whether it fills 30 roles or three.

Above the line, the fixed cost spreads and the per-hire number drops. So the question is not which model is better. It is whether your hiring volume is predictable enough to carry a payroll line. Most organizations get this wrong: overspending of 40% to 60% on recruitment traces back to a sourcing model that does not match actual volume.

Speed muddies the comparison. Agencies fill roles in about 42 days against 63 for in-house teams, cutting three weeks off a vacancy you are paying for either way. Put that in the calculation even when the fee is higher.

### Referral bonuses against a $24,000 placement fee

Referrals are your cheapest external channel by an order of magnitude. The average referral bonus is $2,500. Set that against a 20% fee on a $120,000 engineering hire, which runs $24,000 or more.

Price is not the whole case. Referred hires cost 40% to 60% less than agency placements and stay longer. A cheap hire who leaves in six months was not cheap.

Two things kill referral programs, and both are administrative: slow payment, and a nomination process nobody wants to complete. Pay within 30 days of the new hire's start date and keep the submission to a single form.

## Technical Hires: 23.3 Interview Hours, No Invoice

Everything priced so far arrives as an invoice. The larger half of a hire's cost never does. Soft costs are the time and productivity spent making a hire, and they rarely carry a fixed dollar amount. They are the hours your recruiters, hiring managers and best engineers spend reading resumes, running interviews and scoring candidates instead of doing the job you hired them for.

Interview time is the biggest single line in that half, and it splits sharply by function. A technical hire averages 23.3 total interview hours against 12.2 for a business hire [3]. Data roles run highest at 24.9 and Engineering at 24.7, while Customer Support is lowest at 8.9, a nearly threefold spread [3]. Price those hours at your own loaded rate and you have the multiplier the invoice never shows you.

Treat that as a cost you can cut, not a fixed input. A good share of those rounds are duplicates, run because the hiring side does not trust its own criteria, not because the extra stage adds signal. In high-volume hiring, batching interviews into one scheduled event instead of spreading them across weeks of calendar traffic is the biggest lever you have.

### Applications are up 93% while TA headcount fell 14%

Volume is the second driver, and it moved against you. Application volume is up 93% year over year, while TA teams work with roughly 14% less headcount than they carried in the last hiring boom. More screening, fewer people to absorb it, and none of it visible in a formula built on external spend.

That load lands somewhere. Recruiters spend it triaging resumes instead of building relationships, selling candidates and closing them, which is the work that brings cost per hire down.

### Onboarding and ramp: the spend the formula stops just short of

The formula closes at the accepted offer. Onboarding starts the next day, and it runs $1,500 to $5,000 per employee in the United States, covering training sessions, mentoring time and initial HR processing. Add the ramp before the new hire produces at full rate, plus payroll setup, benefits enrollment and equipment.

None of that counts as cost per hire under the standard definition, and none of it stops being real. Onboarding waste is also the least measured of the preventable cost drivers, because a wrong-fit hire usually reaches day 30 before anyone says it out loud. [Screening for skills](https://testlify.com/technical-skills-assessments-improve-hiring-accuracy/) before you build the shortlist is where that loss gets caught early.

## The $500-a-Day Cost of the Seat You Haven't Filled

The formula's other exclusion sits before the offer, not after it: the cost of not hiring. One open standard role costs $500 a day in lost output. That is not an accounting entry. The work still exists while the seat is empty, so it piles up, lands on teammates already at capacity, or stops getting done.

Put it against a timeline. Median time-to-fill has stretched to 44 days, up from 31 days in 2023, and senior leadership roles average 60-90 days. At the standard rate, 44 days of vacancy costs $22,000 before a single recruiting invoice arrives. A sales seat loses another $7,000 to $10,000 a month in deals that never close.

Delay raises the invoiced side too. Pressure to fill fast pushes teams toward agencies and sponsored listings, the steepest per-hire channels there are. A slow search charges you twice.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should recruiter salaries be included in cost per hire?**

Yes. Include recruiter salaries, benefits and loaded overhead, not just base pay. Spread the TA team's fully loaded cost across the hires made in that period, and use the same period on both sides.

**What is the difference between cost per hire and time to fill?**

Cost per hire prices the spend on a filled seat. Time-to-fill counts the days it took. They move together: median time-to-fill is now 44 days, and each one carries $500 in lost output.

**How do you reduce cost per hire without lowering quality of hire?**

Cut interview hours and vacancy days, not standards. Batch interviews into one event where volume allows, and screen for skills before the shortlist. Cost per hire also drops when a recruiter lowers the bar, so track both.

**Do internal promotions count as hires in cost per hire?**

Only if their recruiting cost goes in the numerator too. Most teams report internal fills separately, because an internal move skips job ads and agency fees and would otherwise flatter the average.
